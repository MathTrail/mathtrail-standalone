# The Model Writes, the Service Checks: Verified Olympiad-Style Maths Problems for Primary-School Children inside Chat Assistants

## Abstract

Olympiad-style problems ask children to work on questions nobody has shown them how to solve, and such practice needs a steady supply of problems that are new to each child. A chat assistant can write them without end. Its problems, however, can carry a wrong answer key, or two correct options, or none, and a child cannot tell a faulty key from their own mistake. We present MathTrail, an open-source service for grades 1–6 that runs inside chat assistants and splits the work in two. The chat's own model writes every task; a small deterministic service proposes what the child practises next and admits a task only when none of nine checks finds a fault in it. The central check runs a program the model wrote alongside the task: in a sandbox it must single out exactly one of the five options, and the same one again when the option labels are rotated. The other checks test the task's structure, the explanations of its wrong options, readability for the task's level, near-duplicates of reference tasks and of the child's own history, and its text drawing. The answer stays sealed until the child answers. A Rasch model with a guessing floor, on one ladder of difficulty across grades 1–6, places a new child with a short trial series and then, updated Elo-style, aims practice at a corridor of 70–85 % expected success. We show that, when the model is right, its update aims at the same topic levels as maximum likelihood, with longer steps, the longest after tasks harder than the corridor. The service makes no model calls of its own. In an offline evaluation, 23,453 defects injected into reference tasks were refused whenever they broke a rule a check states, while a second correct option written in words always passed and a copy with new numbers passed about one time in twenty [ea1]; in simulated learners the service estimated a child who stays put as well as any baseline but fell a logit behind a child who learns steadily, and most of its declarations of mastery came while the child solved the level's middle task below the corridor's target rate [ea3]. We release the service and its 603 reference tasks, each with its solver.

## 1 Introduction

School mathematics is best at procedures: how to find a percentage, how to solve an equation of a known form. Reviewing the national assessment of the United States, the National Research Council found 13-year-olds taught this way more fluent in procedures than able to formulate and solve problems or to reason about them [@anonymous2001adding]. Solving a problem one has never seen is a different skill, although it rests on the same fluency. It means understanding the problem, devising a plan, carrying it out and looking back, with moves such as trying a special case or working backwards [@polya2014solve]. It also means watching one's own progress and dropping an approach that leads nowhere [@schoenfeld2016learning]. Olympiad-style problems call for exactly this skill. They come from a long tradition of mathematical circles and competitions [@delosada2022perspectives; @leikin2021practice], whose effects have seldom been studied systematically [@leikin2021practice; @rebholz2022getting]. In the one study with a comparison group we found in primary school, selected third- and fourth-graders taught in a group course did better than the comparison group in the competition and, in grade 4, on a curriculum test; the groups were not randomised and differed before the course [@rebholz2022getting]. In our working definition, a problem is olympiad-style when its method is not immediately obvious to the solver [@oecd2013pisa], its solution rests on an idea rather than a procedure, its condition needs no knowledge beyond the child's grade, and, once the idea is found, the solution is short [Q15]. Such practice has a demanding supply chain: every problem should be new to the child, a little harder than the last one, and still solvable.

Large language models can supply such problems without end, and a chat assistant puts them one message away. What a model writes, though, cannot go to a child unchecked. Evaluations of models as maths tutors find frequent mistakes: when models tutored college algebra, only 56.6 % of 150 tutoring dialogues were entirely correct [@gupta2025beyond]. Teachers who judge the word problems models write find part of them unsolvable, wrongly answered or unfit for school, even from the strongest models [@christ2024mathwell; @christ2026edumath]. For a child the cost of such a fault is high: a wrong answer key turns a correct solution into a "mistake", and a child has no way to tell which of the two is at fault.

We take the view that the writing and the checking should be done by different parties. The chat's own model, which already talks to the child, writes each task. A small service that makes no model calls of its own proposes what the child should practise, hands the model what it needs to write the task, and admits the task only after it has checked everything that can be checked mechanically. The service then shows the task without its answer and records the child's answer. This paper describes that service, MathTrail, and asks two questions:

- **RQ1.** Which defects in a model-written task do the service's checks catch, and which do they miss?
- **RQ2.** Does the learner model place a new child and keep the child's practice in its target corridor, and how often does it declare a topic mastered when it is not?

Our contributions are:

1. **An architecture** in which the chat's model writes every task and a stateless service verifies it, keeps the answer sealed until the child answers, and makes no model calls of its own (Section 3).
2. **A verification pipeline** of nine checks. At its centre is a solver the model writes alongside the task, which the service runs twice in a sandbox under rotated option labels. Near-duplicates are detected without keeping the text of past tasks (Section 4).
3. **A learner model and selection rule** for tasks used once: the hierarchical Elo model of adaptive practice, with a guessing floor, on a designed ladder of difficulty across grades 1–6 in place of item difficulties learned from other learners, and a trial series that places a new child by the most likely level given all its answers before the updates take over. We relate the update to maximum likelihood and state the mastery rule precisely (Section 5).
4. **An open artifact**: the service's code under the MIT license, and 603 reference tasks for grades 1–6, each with a solver and with a trap named behind each wrong option [C044, C077] (Section 3).
5. **An offline evaluation** by fault injection and simulation, before any child is involved (Section 6). Every injected defect that breaks a rule a check states was refused, while a second correct option written in words always passed and short computations given again with new numbers often did [ea1]; the learner model estimated a child who stays put as well as any baseline, but it is slow to follow a child who learns, and most declarations of its mastery rule come while the child solves the level's middle task below the corridor's target rate [ea3].

Section 2 places the work among related work. Sections 3, 4 and 5 describe the system, its checks and its learner model, Section 6 evaluates them, and Sections 7 and 8 discuss what the service cannot do, its ethics and what follows.

## 2 Related Work

**Generating maths problems.** Language models have written word problems for kindergarten to grade 8, each answered by a program the model wrote and judged by teachers for solvability, accuracy and appropriateness [@christ2024mathwell]; problems aligned with the standards of grades 3–5 and tried with pupils [@christ2026edumath]; routine exercises for grades 1–6, without answers [@ariyarathne2025elementary]; questions hard enough to defeat the models themselves [@shah2024aiassisted; @patel2025get]; and the wrong options of teachers' multiple-choice questions, each meant to reveal a misconception [@feng2024exploring; @fernandez2024divert]. Among the works we read, none writes olympiad-style problems for primary school, and none admits a problem by running a program its author wrote against the problem's options. The nearest are these: GSM-Symbolic computes each key with a program, but people write the program [@mirzadeh2024gsmsymbolic]; Beast Academy offers olympiad-style problems for ages 6–13, written by people [@beastacademy2026]; Spiral Learning serves olympiad problems to ages 7–13 from a bank whose origin its page does not state [@spirallearning2026]; and MathForces generates olympiad quizzes with a model, for an audience it does not state and with no check it describes [@mathforces2026].

**Checking them.** A model is a weak judge of problems. A model evaluating problems for grades 1–6 agreed poorly with people on whether a problem could be solved [@ariyarathne2025elementary]; people still edited most of the hard questions that frontier models had validated themselves [@shah2024aiassisted]; and a model reviewing its own reasoning without outside feedback rarely finds its mistakes [@huang2023large; @tyen2024llms]. Models also often answer an underspecified problem instead of flagging it [@li2025questbench; @kirichenko2025abstentionbench], and the best model tested, GPT-4, detected unanswerable school word problems less reliably than people did [@sun2024benchmarking]. A question can be checked step by step by models [@shen2026lets] or validated by agents, each a model judging one criterion, solvability included [@ikram2026multiagent], and a model's output can be judged by a jury of models rather than one [@verga2024replacing]. A program can compute the answer instead [@gao2022pal; @chen2022program], and a model can check its own answer with code it writes, reading the result itself [@zhou2023solving]. We take the program route without a second model, since the service calls none: the service runs code the model wrote and compares the result with the model's own key.

**Repetition.** The answers language models give to open-ended requests resemble each other, within one model and across models [@jiang2025artificial], although how strongly is disputed [@schaeffer2026strong]. Near-duplicates are found by the overlap of shingle sets [@broder1997resemblance], estimated from min-wise sketches [@broder2000minwise], as the deduplication of training data still does [@lee2022deduplicating]. Such a check sees repeated text, not a repeated idea: a paraphrase passes it [@toshniwal2024openmathinstruct]. Ours checks repeated text, against the reference tasks of the task's level and the child's last 200 tasks.

**Learner models and difficulty.** Our response model is Rasch's logistic model [@rasch1961general] with the guessing floor of Birnbaum's three-parameter model [@birnbaum1968some]. Its update is the Elo rating of adaptive practice [@pelanek2016applications] in its hierarchical form: a general level corrected by a topic level, a floor for multiple choice, and a step that shrinks with the number of answers [@niznan2015student; @pelanek2017elobased]. Math Garden, the best-known system of this kind, estimates ability and difficulty on the fly and chooses items for an expected success of 0.75 [@klinkenberg2011computer]. For learners trained by gradient descent on two-way choices, theory puts the fastest learning at an accuracy of about 85 % for Gaussian noise, and at 82 % or 75 % for two other kinds of noise [@wilson2019eighty]; for a child choosing among five options this is an analogy, not a result. Unlike these systems, ours never reuses a task, so a task's difficulty cannot be learned from other children's answers. It has to be asked for, and it is the model's writing that must deliver it. Knowledge tracing fits its parameters to earlier learners' answers to recurring skills or items [@corbett1995knowledge; @piech2015deep]; the service keeps no data across children, each profile living in its own parent's Drive [C078], so there is nothing to fit them to. The general Bayesian trackers that give the step a principled size [@glickman1999parameter; @herbrich2007trueskill; @bolsinova2022urnings] have, as published, no guessing floor; for Glicko and TrueSkill, adding one has been called difficult [@pelanek2016applications], and Section 6.3 runs a Glicko-2 with a floor of our own construction; a Glicko variant for learner models takes the time between a learner's answers into its confidence in a rating [@abdi2021modelling].

**Tutors inside chat assistants.** Unguarded chat help can harm learning: in a high-school trial, students who had practised with a plain GPT-4 chat did worse on a later exam than students who never had it, and a tutor given the solutions and told not to give answers removed the harm without adding a gain [@bastani2025generative]. Where tutoring with a model did help, correctness and structure came from outside the model: instructors' solutions in the prompt [@kestin2025ai], hints checked by a person [@pardos2024chatgptgenerated], or a validated key, a named misconception and an expert tutor approving every message [@learnlmteam2025ai]. We found no trial of a model that writes tasks for children of primary age, and pooled evidence for primary pupils is thin [@wu2026chatgpts]. The major assistants now offer study modes that change how the model talks, holding back answers and asking questions; none of their pages describes a check of the problems they pose [@openai2025study; @anthropic2025education; @google2025guided]. Khan Academy brings questions from its bank, written by people, into ChatGPT for teachers [@khan2026chatgpt]. An open-source MCP tutor separates what the model writes from a rule-driven engine of progression, as we do, but describes no check of the tasks it writes [@guiovanna2026tutor]. Hosts let third-party services add tools and widgets through the Model Context Protocol and its MCP Apps extension [@mcp2026spec; @mcpapps2026; @anthropic2026customconnectors; @openai2026plugins]. A language model alone predicted students' next answers worse than a dedicated learner model, and its authors recommend pairing a model that writes with a model that tracks the learner [@hooshyar2026large]. Our service keeps the learner model's rule and its update to itself: a deterministic rule proposes each task, and the model may depart from it only with a stated reason, which the service records [C018, C083]; the model still reports the answers the child gives in the chat [C039]. The service checks what the model writes and never shows the answer on the child's card before the child answers, leaving the model the part it does well: writing.

## 3 The System

### 3.1 Design principles

Each mechanism of the service serves a learning goal (Table 1).

**Table 1.** Learning goals and the mechanisms that serve them. Status: *built* — at the pinned commit the code and its tests show it.

| Learning goal | Mechanism | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Every problem is new to the child, on ideas that recur | The chat's model writes each task for this child; a near-duplicate check, which compares text, rejects copies of the reference tasks and repeats of the child's own past tasks; topics and traps come back | built [C019, C020, C027, C028] |
| Difficulty "slightly harder" | The rule asks for the point of the ladder whose predicted success is closest to 0.775, the middle of a corridor of 0.70–0.85; a trial series first finds where the child stands | built [C014, C018, C080] |
| Mistakes that can be diagnosed | Each wrong option embodies a trap from a closed catalog and carries its own explanation of the mistake | built [C020, C043, C072] |
| The answer is not given away | The answer, the explanations, the solution and the solver are sealed until the child answers, and the card never shows them before | built [C033, C064] |
| Help without the answer | A hint — one leading question or the first step — which the card keeps folded until the child opens it | built [C061, C074] |
| Readable at the task's level | Sentence length by grade level; Flesch–Kincaid for English | built [C030] |
| A key the child can trust | The model's own solver must single out the keyed option, twice | built [C023] |

### 3.2 Who does what

Figure 1 follows one task through the design; every step of it is built [Figure 1: research/paper-a/figures/flow.tex. Caption: One task, from the request to the answer, on the path where it is accepted. The chat's model writes the task and the service checks it; a refused task goes back to the model with every problem found (dashed). The service reads the child's profile from the parent's Drive when a call needs it and writes back what changed, keeping nothing of the child between calls; the answer stays sealed in the profile until the child has answered, on the card or in the chat. The card is not shown the answer, but the host hands it the arguments of the call that submitted the task, the key among them.]

1. **The rule proposes.** When the child asks for a task, a deterministic rule reads the child's profile and builds a brief: the goal, the topic, the grade level and difficulty on one ladder of difficulty, the setting and two traps [C018]. It sets only topics within the child's reach, and a child below every topic gets those taught from grades 1–2, where the ladder begins [C081]. During a new child's trial series each task takes a new topic; after that, a wrong answer makes the goal to reinforce the topic of that answer while the topic stays within reach, and otherwise the rule moves to a new topic: an unmastered topic never given yet, or else the one given longest ago [C019]. The two traps are the child's most frequent in the topic, topped up with the most frequent among the topic's reference tasks of the task's level, then of the levels nearest it [C020]. The model may ask for another topic, level or difficulty; it must say why, and the brief keeps both its reason and the rule's [C083].
2. **The service equips the model.** With the brief the model receives a package: the corridor, the topic, every trap of the catalog, the skills the task may not use, the child's grade and interests and the parent's notes [C092], three reference tasks, the topic's solver templates and drawing frames, the limits the task is held to, and a guide to writing it [C073, C046]. The package carries no pseudonym [C073]. A test holds every package a profile allows to a budget of 64 KiB, so nothing has to be dropped to fit [C008]; Section 6.4 gives the packages' sizes. The package also carries the version of the instructions, a prefix of a SHA-256 over them [C007].
3. **The model writes.** It hands in the task with its self-check and the source of its solver. The task gives the question, five options, the key, a hint, a step-by-step solution and, for each of the four wrong options, the trap it comes from and what the child reads after choosing it [C072, C061]. A text drawing is optional.
4. **The service checks.** Section 4 describes the nine checks. A refused task goes back to the model with every problem found. The model gets at most three attempts per request [C032].
5. **The child sees the task, not the answer.** The answer and everything that would reveal it are sealed with XChaCha20-Poly1305, under a subkey of their own and bound to this child and this task, until the child answers [C033]. The card shows the question, the drawing, the options and the hint, and nothing that gives the answer away [C064]. Hosts hand a card the arguments of the call that drew it [@mcpapps2026], and the task's card is drawn by the call that submitted the task [C096], so the correct answer sits unseen in the card's memory; the host's own tool log, which an adult can open, shows it too [C064].
6. **The answer comes back** through a tool the card calls itself, or the model calls when the child answers in the chat, and it is recorded once [C039]. The service then unseals the task, marks the answer, shows the explanation of the mistake behind a wrong option, and updates the ratings (Section 5).

The service keeps nothing of a child between requests; all it holds is each instance's count of requests for its rate limits [C041]. It is a single Go binary with no database, running on Cloud Run [C001, C002, C005]. The child's profile is a JSON file in the parent's own Google Drive, reached with the parent's token [C078]. The parent signs in through the service's own OAuth authorization server, with Google, which is asked only for who the parent is and for the files the service itself creates; what a sign-in needs is carried sealed, never stored [C040]. The service speaks the Model Context Protocol, in its revision of 2026-07-28, over HTTP without sessions [C062] through seven tools [C038]. Every card is one MCP Apps page whose content security policy names no outside domain, which MCP Apps reads as no external connections [C068; @mcpapps2026]. The service calls no language model, and no model provider's SDK is among its dependencies: every task is written by the model the family already talks to, within the family's own use of the chat, so the service's operator pays for computation alone (Section 6.4) [C003]. Privacy follows from the same design: the service keeps no copy of the profile, which lives in the parent's Drive [C002, C078]; its log lines carry no task, answer or pseudonym [C036]; and the answer stays sealed until the child answers (Section 4.6). The content — catalogs, reference tasks, instructions, schemas, solver templates and drawing frames — is embedded in the binary [C006].

### 3.3 Content

The catalogs hold 17 topics, 20 traps and 25 skills [C043]. A trap names a typical mistake, such as answering a different question from the one asked, or counting one case twice. A skill names a piece of knowledge a child may not have been taught yet; the skills a parent excludes reach the model as what the task may not use [C073]. The service ships 603 reference tasks: 200 for grades 1–2, 250 for grades 3–4 and 153 for grades 5–6, each with a solver of its own [C044]. Behind each wrong option of every reference task stands a trap of the catalog [C077].

The reference tasks of grades 1–4 come from the service's prototype. Claude wrote them at the author's request, in the genre of Soviet collections of olympiad and graded problems. Each answer was confirmed by a brute-force function and by a blind solve by another model, and the author reviewed every task [P012, P013]. Those of grades 5–6 were written by Claude as well, in ten batches, each checked by the author [C066]. The model is also given 33 solver templates and 11 drawing frames to start from [C045].

### 3.4 What is built

We describe the service at a pinned commit. Everything described above is built: the rule, the checks, the solver's sandbox, the learner model with its trial series, the sealing and the content, and around them the MCP endpoint and its tools, sign-in, the profile in Drive, the limits on use and the card, whose words exist in 22 languages [C004, C038–C041, C068, C078, C087]. The product has 1,099 test functions, 32 fuzz tests and 99 property tests, with 82.1 % statement coverage [C047, C048]. Where the prototype had reference numbers — the ratings' worked example, readability, similarity and the rule's briefs — the service is tested against vectors exported by the prototype's own code, and no test may rewrite them [C059, C060].

Earlier builds of the service have run with a real model. Locally and in text mode, Claude Sonnet 5 wrote 15 tasks over 17 hand-ins; 13 were accepted at the first attempt, and each of the 15, solved by hand, had the keyed answer as its intended one, though one could also be read another way (Section 4.5) [C084]. On the deployed service, in Claude's web client, a child's trial series of five tasks went through, each task accepted at its first attempt [C085]. These runs show that the path works end to end; they are too few to measure anything, and the checks have changed a little since. Acceptance in ChatGPT, and measurements on the deployed service, are still to come [C090]. Our evaluation (Section 6) therefore runs offline, on the service's own code, where every number can be reproduced by a script.

## 4 Verification

### 4.1 Nine checks

A submitted task is either admitted or refused with one or more of nine codes (Table 2) [C021]: it is admitted exactly when no check finds a problem, so admission is the conjunction of the nine checks. The review runs in two halves. The first decodes the task and runs its solver before the child's profile is read; the second runs every check whose input is present. A check whose input is missing is reported as not run rather than passed, and every input a check can lack — a part that can be read, the question, the structure of a drawing — is one the structure check requires, so such a task is refused in any case [C022]. Every problem is reported at once, so the model can fix all of them in its next attempt. No message quotes an option's letter or text, because a refusal reaches the child's widget with everything else the tool returns [C071].

**Table 2.** The nine checks and what each refuses.

| Code | Refuses |
|---|---|
| `bad_structure` | a task out of format, or one that does not match the brief |
| `distractor_explanations` | an explanation of a wrong option that cannot tell a child what went wrong: a copy, or too short |
| `drawing_format` | a text drawing wider than 30 cells or taller than 12 lines, or with characters that fonts draw differently [C031] |
| `drawing_mismatch` | a drawing that does not show what its declared structure says, or a structure that misses a label the wording uses |
| `readability` | a question too hard to read for the task's level |
| `solver_error` | a solver that does not run to an answer |
| `solver_disagrees` | a solver that finds no option, more than one, or another than the key; or a self-check that reaches another answer |
| `self_check_blocking` | a task whose own self-check reports a blocking issue |
| `near_duplicate` | a question that copies a reference task of the task's level or repeats one of the child's past tasks |

### 4.2 The solver, run twice

The model writes, with each task, a short program in Starlark, a deterministic dialect of Python. The program computes the answer from the problem's data and returns the letters of the options whose text means that value. The service runs it in a sandbox limited to 25 million steps and two seconds, one run per processor at a time; a run that finds no free slot within three seconds does not start, and the model is told its task was not checked and no attempt was spent [C024]. Memory is bounded through the steps: everything the sandbox predeclares pays a step for every sixteen bytes it builds, so the built-ins of one run keep at most 381 MiB [C079]. Memory the language takes by itself is not priced: a program that doubles a list with the language's own operators reaches about 480 MiB in under 300 steps, and a loop of empty dictionary literals killed an instance of 1 GiB [C075]. Under that price the costliest of the 603 reference solvers takes 2,413,617 steps, under a tenth of the ceiling [C025]. The run must single out exactly one option, the keyed one. Two options that say the same thing are a fault of the task, and the service does not pick one of them quietly.

A program can defeat such a check by returning a letter written out by hand. The service therefore runs the program a second time on the same five option texts, with their labels moved by two: the text labelled A becomes C, B becomes D, and so on [C023]. A program that computes its answer follows the text to its new label. One that writes out a letter returns the old label and fails. No option keeps its label, so a letter written out by hand now names another text. The rotation removes the laziest failure, not every failure: a program that writes out the value, rather than the letter, still passes.

### 4.3 Near-duplicates without keeping the text

A question is a near-duplicate of another when the Jaccard index of their character trigrams, their resemblance [@broder1997resemblance], is at least 0.7. The trigrams are built from padded words as PostgreSQL's `pg_trgm` builds them, and scripts written without spaces use character bigrams instead [C027]. For bigrams the threshold is the same 0.7, reasoned rather than measured [C076]. The question is compared with the reference questions of the task's level and with the child's own past tasks [C028]. Past tasks are not stored as text. The profile keeps a MinHash sketch [@broder2000minwise] of 192 positions, each minimum cut to four bits, 96 bytes, for each of the last 200 tasks at most [C029]; those papers do not treat such a cut, so its error is measured here. Over every pair of reference questions of one level, 62,653 pairs among all 603, the sketch misses the exact index by 0.031 on average and by 0.139 at worst. It decides 33 of those pairs differently from the exact index at the 0.7 threshold, all of them within 0.1 of it [C050].

### 4.4 Readability

Text that models write for young children reads harder than text people write for them, and naming the age in the prompt changes little [@valentini2023automatic], so the service checks readability rather than trusting the brief. The longest sentence of a question may have at most 20, 25 or 30 words for a task of grade level 1–2, 3–4 or 5–6. The limits follow the level the task is written for, not the child's grade. In English, the Flesch–Kincaid grade may exceed the youngest grade of the level by at most three [C030]. For scripts written without spaces the limits count characters, at twice the word limits, a guess nobody has measured [C076]. On the reference tasks, the whole check passes 76 % of the grades 1–2 tasks and 91 % of the grades 3–4 tasks at the limits of their own levels [C049]. For grades 5–6, added later, it passes all 153 [ea1].

### 4.5 What the checks cannot see

An admitted task carries one guarantee about its key: the model's own program, run on the five option texts under both labellings, singles out the keyed option and no other [C023]. The key is thus right relative to the model's formalisation of the problem, and the checks test that formalisation, not the meaning of its text [C026]. The solver is written by the same model that wrote the question. If the model misreads its own question, and the solver computes the misreading and the key agrees with it, every check passes. A program removes a model's arithmetic errors, not its misreadings: in one analysis of failures on financial questions, the programs had mostly bound wrong values to the problem's quantities or computed the wrong thing [@chen2022program], and a solver written by the task's author shares the author's reading of the task. The self-check asks the model what benchmarks of ill-posed problems ask — whether a condition is missing, the question can be read two ways, a negation is easy to miss, a word or a range is vague, conditions contradict each other, or the right option answers another question [C095] — but it is the same model's second opinion, not an independent one, and a model rarely finds its own mistakes [@huang2023large; @tyen2024llms]. Faced with a problem that is underspecified or vague, models tend to answer definitively rather than flag it [@kirichenko2025abstentionbench]: told that a shop gave a discount of more than $8 a shirt, GPT-4 subtracted $8 and computed a definite answer [@sun2024benchmarking]. A check can even push a task towards such a fault: in the first local run, the model shortened a question to pass the readability check and dropped the word "different", on which the answer rested, and the task it handed in could be read two ways; no check saw it [C089]. We return to these limits in Section 7. Section 6.2 measures them: injected defects of these kinds passed, unless a check tripped over the changed wording.

### 4.6 The answer's path

Sealing is a property of where the answer can flow. The service writes the answer, the explanations of the wrong options, the solution and the solver into one field of the profile, sealed with XChaCha20-Poly1305 under a subkey of their own and bound to this child and this task, and, once answered, to the letter given [C033]. Before the child answers, no result the service returns to the card holds any of them [C064], and no log line, trace or metric holds the task's text, its options or an answer letter, which tests check [C036]. The seal opens in one place: the result that records the child's answer. Two copies stay outside this path, both named in Section 7: the model that wrote the task knows its answer, and the host hands the card the arguments of the call that submitted the task, which hold the key, and lists them in its own tool log [C096, C064].

## 5 Learner Model and Selection Rule

### 5.1 The model

The service predicts the chance that a child answers a task correctly as

$$P = c + (1 - c)\,\sigma(\theta + \delta_t - \beta), \qquad c = 0.2, \qquad \beta = s_\ell + (d - 3),$$

where θ is the child's overall level and δ_t the child's offset for the task's topic t [C009]. A task is written for a grade level ℓ — grades 1–2, 3–4 or 5–6 — and a difficulty d from 1 to 5 inside it, the ones the brief asked for. The shifts s_ℓ = 0, 2.5 and 5 put all fifteen points on one ladder, on which the two hardest difficulties of a level overlap the two easiest of the next; the step of 2.5 is a guess nothing has measured yet [C010]. The floor c is the chance of guessing one of five options with no penalty. The model is one-dimensional in θ with one extra dimension per topic, each task loading on both. In adaptive practice this is the hierarchical Elo model, a global skill corrected by a concept skill, there with item difficulties learned from many learners [@pelanek2017elobased]; ours are set by the ladder. The child sees each level as a chess-style number, R = 1500 + (400/ln 10)·level, on one scale for every child, with one of eleven ranks, each a corridor wide [C016].

After the trial series of Section 5.4, both levels move Elo-style after each answer S ∈ {0, 1}:

$$\theta \leftarrow \theta + K_\theta (S - P), \qquad \delta_t \leftarrow \delta_t + K_\delta (S - P), \qquad K = \frac{K_0}{1 + 0.05\,n}.$$

For θ, K₀ = 0.2 and n counts the child's answers so far, the trial answers included. For δ_t, K₀ = 0.4 and n counts the answers so far in topic t [C011]. Only correctness enters the update, and "I don't know" counts as a wrong answer [C012]. The difficulty β enters as a given and is never updated or stored: each task is written for one child and never handed out again [C013].

### 5.2 How the update relates to maximum likelihood

The log-likelihood of one answer is ℓ = S ln P + (1 − S) ln(1 − P). With x = θ + δ_t − β, its gradient is

$$\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial x} = (S - P)\, w(P), \qquad w(P) = \frac{P - c}{P\,(1 - c)} .$$

The service's step K(S − P) is therefore the gradient step divided by w(P). When the model is right — when every task's true chance of success is the P the model gives it at the child's true levels — the two have the same expected zero: both stop moving, on average, where each topic's level θ + δ_t is the child's true one, and dropping w adds no bias. Answers identify only these sums; how each sum splits between the shared θ and the topic's δ_t follows from the steps, not from the answers. What it changes is the size of the step. Under a wrong model the two may settle at different levels, because the gradient weighs tasks by w(P) and the service weighs them all alike. In simulation they settled together for children whose slope or guessing floor differ from the model's (Section 6.3) [ea3]. At the corridor's ends, P = 0.85 and P = 0.70, the service steps 4.6 % and 12 % further than the gradient; at P = 0.5 a third further, and at P = 0.3 2.4 times as far. Above the corridor the difference fades. The level thus moves further, and more noisily, after tasks harder than the corridor. On the ladder such tasks are rare but not absent. A topic taught from grades 1–2 whose level stands below −1.49 has no task easy enough, since even the easiest point of the ladder then gives P < 0.70; the rule sets such a topic only when no topic is within reach. The corridor, 0.96 wide on the β scale, sometimes holds no point of a topic, and the nearest point then lies just outside it. And the model may choose a harder task itself [C014, C081, C083].

The step shrinks as 1/(1 + 0.05n), the uncertainty function of Elo-based adaptive practice [@papousek2014adaptive], a sequence of type 1/n in the sense of Robbins and Monro [@robbins1951stochastic]. For one topic, with every task served at exactly P = 0.775 on a continuous scale, the rule is their recursion for the point of the child's response curve where success is 0.775: it converges there in probability, whatever the curve's shape, provided the curve never falls as tasks get easier and crosses 0.775 with a positive slope, and the child does not change. The ladder's fifteen points, the corridor and the level θ shared across topics make this an idealisation. For a child whose ability stays put, the estimate settles. A child who learns is followed more and more slowly, because v1 has no floor under the step, such as Math Garden now has [@vermeiren2025balancing] and Glicko's author recommends [@glickman2016glicko]; and with a constant step, difficulties fixed and a target above one half, Elo's estimates were found to run low [@bolsinova2025keeping]. In simulation, after 200 answers at the service's target, the shrinking step's estimate stood 0.08 logits below the truth and the constant step's 0.03 below; with every task chosen at a predicted 0.5 they stood 0.16 and 0.22 above. Raising the target lowered both, in the direction found for the constant step [@bolsinova2025keeping], but from above the truth rather than from it, so in our setting a target above one half is not the bias's only source; a child who learned steadily was left 1.1 logits behind (Section 6.3) [ea3].

### 5.3 The corridor and mastery

The target is a predicted success in the corridor [0.70, 0.85]. Inverting the model of Section 5.1, the corridor is an interval of difficulty,

$$\beta \in [\,\theta + \delta_t - 1.4663,\; \theta + \delta_t - 0.5108\,],$$

0.96 wide, narrower than the step of 1 between two difficulties of a level, so it holds at most one difficulty of a level, and sometimes none; where two levels overlap, their points stand 0.5 apart and it can hold two. The brief therefore asks for the point of the topic whose P is closest to the middle, 0.775, the chance at β = θ + δ_t − 0.9383, measured as a distance in P, not in β; of two points equally close on either side of the middle, the easier; whether or not it lies inside [C014]. The rule is thus a deterministic map from the profile to a brief. Outside the trial series, after a wrong answer it keeps the topic of that answer while the topic is within reach; otherwise, and throughout the trial series, it takes the unmastered topic within reach that has waited longest, or, once every topic within reach is mastered, the one of them that has waited longest, ties going to the catalog's order; and it sets the topic's point whose P is nearest 0.775 (Section 3.2) [C019, C070, C081]. The same profile always gets the same brief [C018], unless the model asks for another, with a reason [C083]. A topic counts as mastered once it has at least five answers and, since its last wrong answer, at least three qualifying answers. A qualifying answer is correct, on a task whose P was at most 0.775, given without the hint. A correct answer that does not qualify neither adds to the run nor breaks it. Mastery is lost after two wrong answers in a row [C015]. A mastered topic leaves the rotation until every topic within reach is mastered [C070]. Mastery is held at a level: once the rule's recommended point in a topic moves to the level above, the topic is back in the rotation until the child masters it there too [C082]. A run of correct answers is a simple mastery rule often used in practice; tuned, it does nearly as well as knowledge tracing [@pelanek2017experimental]. Three in a row predicted the next two answers well, though five predicted a transfer task better [@kelly2015defining]. A run assumes a learner who never slips [@doroudi2020mastery], whereas our tasks are set where a child fails about one time in four or five, the noisy case in which a run is weakest [@pelanek2018conceptual]. The gate on P and the five answers are what our rule sets against that. And a streak is not learning: in a large trial, a rule of three correct in a row raised the platform's own count of mastery but not what students retained on a delayed test [@oreopoulos2026making]. We report mastery as the rule's state, not as what the child has learned.

A false "mastered" needs three qualifying answers without a wrong one between them. Take the attempts on eligible tasks of a topic — those whose P was at most 0.775 — and a child who answers such a task correctly without the hint with probability s, wrongly with probability f, and correctly with the hint otherwise. The run is then a Markov chain on the states 0, 1, 2 and an absorbing state 3: a correct answer without the hint moves one state up, a wrong answer returns to 0, and a correct answer with the hint leaves the state as it is. A wrong answer on any other task of the topic also returns the run to 0, so, for a child whose true chance is the same on every eligible task, the chain bounds from above the chance of being declared mastered within m eligible attempts. At a true chance of 0.7 and no hints it reaches three within 5 eligible attempts with probability 0.55 and within 10 with 0.86; at 0.6, with 0.39 and 0.70; at 0.8, within 10 attempts, with 0.96 [ea3]. So a child whose true chance is 0.6, well below the corridor's middle, may be declared mastered within ten eligible attempts with a chance of about 0.70, an upper bound the chain gives.

### 5.4 Placement: the grade sets only the start

The grade the parent enters says where a child starts on the ladder, not where the child belongs: a new child begins at θ₀ = s_ℓ of the level the grade falls into, and a grade changed later moves no rating [C065]. The step of Section 5.1 is made for a level that is roughly right already, and a child placed a level too high would fail task after task while it walked down. So the first five answers are a trial series. Each of its tasks takes a topic the child has not met yet [C019], and after each answer θ is set to the most likely level given the start and every trial answer at once:

$$\hat\theta = \arg\max_\theta \Big[ -\frac{(\theta - \theta_0)^2}{2\sigma_0^2} + \sum_{i} \big( S_i \ln P(\theta - \beta_i) + (1 - S_i) \ln (1 - P(\theta - \beta_i)) \big) \Big], \qquad \sigma_0 = 2.5,$$

a normal prior around the start, one level shift wide, times the chance of each answer under the model of Section 5.1. The topic offsets do not move during the series: a trial answer says where the child stands, not what the child knows of one topic [C080]. Every estimate is made afresh from all the trial answers, so an early slip is weighed again at every later answer; the aim is that a child placed a level too high meets easier tasks from the second or third task on. The posterior can have two peaks, so the service searches a grid over the whole range the peak can lie in before it narrows the best cell. The spread σ₀ was chosen by simulation, as a balance between a child whose grade was entered a level off and one whose grade was right [C080]. After the series, the updates of Section 5.1 take over.

Topics open by reach rather than by grade: the rule sets a topic only when its easiest task has P ≥ 0.70 for the child, or, when no topic is within reach, those taught from grades 1–2 [C081]. Readability follows the task's level rather than the child's grade [C030]. Section 6.3 measures how well the series places a child whose true level lies a grade level below or above the start: after five answers it stood 0.94 logits from such a child's level on average, against 2.24 for the update alone [ea3].

### 6.1 Method

All experiments run on the service's own code at the pinned commit, with no child and no language model involved. ### 6.2 RQ1: fault injection

**Design.** We take reference tasks with their solvers and inject one defect at a time. The classes are fixed in the experiment's protocol before any run, each marked as one the checks must catch or as out of scope; within a class a few operators make a defect a child would see that no check is defined by, which the protocol marks as a discovery. Those the checks must catch: a wrong key; two correct options, or none; a solver that fails, runs out of steps, or writes out a letter; a self-check that disagrees or reports a blocking issue; a broken structure, or a task that does not match its brief; a trap outside the catalog; an explanation of a wrong option that repeats the solution or is empty; a question too hard to read for its level; a copy of a reference question, or a repeat of the child's own past task; a drawing out of size, with forbidden characters or with undeclared labels. Those out of scope — ambiguous wording, a missing condition, a solver that computes a misreading the key agrees with, a trap that names the wrong mistake, a hint that gives the answer away, the pseudonym in the text, a task off its topic or its difficulty, and the same idea in a new setting — are included to measure the limits of Section 4.5, not to count as misses. A reference question is compared with the reference set without itself. This design does not measure false refusals: the thresholds were set on the reference tasks, so what they refuse among them shows how the limits were chosen rather than how often a good new task is refused, which needs good tasks the checks were never tuned on [TBD: E-A2, if run].

**Measures.** For every class, the share of injected defects refused, and by which checks, with 95 % intervals. For every check, the share of cases it alone refused: what switching it off would lose. Each operator applies to every reference task it fits, and the protocol, with its classes and operators, was frozen and timestamped before the first run [ea1].

**Results.** Of the 603 reference tasks, 447 pass every check as they are: 126 of 200 for grades 1–2, 168 of 250 for grades 3–4 and all 153 for grades 5–6. The others read too hard for their own level or as near-copies of another reference task of it, and no defect was injected into them. On the 447, the 68 operators of 27 classes made 23,453 cases [ea1].

Every case of the 53 operators that break a rule some check states was refused, by the check expected: a wrong key, two correct options written alike, no correct option, a solver that fails, runs out of steps or writes out a letter, a self-check that disagrees or blocks, a broken structure, a brief that does not match the request, an unknown trap, a hollow explanation, a sentence too long or words too hard, a verbatim or reordered copy, a drawing out of size, with forbidden characters or with labels that disagree. With up to 447 cases an operator, every 95 % interval starts at 96.8 % or higher, except those of the drawing operators, which only the 23 or 24 tasks with a drawing take, at 85 %. All but two of these operators were refused by one check alone; the right answer written twice is refused by both the structure check and the solver, and an explanation put under the right option by both the structure check and the check of explanations. Switching off any one check would let its defects through, all but those two kinds, which the other check still refuses.

Three operators make a defect a child would see that no check is defined by (Table 3). A copy of a reference task with every number raised by one was refused in 94.6 % of 332 cases (95 % interval 91.6–96.8 %), and a repeat of the child's own task with new numbers in 96.1 % of 357 (93.5–97.8 %); every miss was a task of one topic, arithmetic tricks — 18 of its 39 copies and 14 of its 39 repeats passed — whose questions are short sums and products in which the numbers make up most of the text. A second correct option, the right whole number written in words beside the digits, was never refused in 213 cases (0–1.7 %): the solver matches an option by its text, and nothing reads "six" as 6.

The out-of-scope defects mostly went through, as expected. A hint that gives the answer, a trap that names the wrong mistake, and a well-formed task of another topic, or two difficulties away, under the brief were never refused. An exact quantity made vague or a range, a removed condition, a question turned to ask the opposite — the misreading the solver and the key share — and the pseudonym in the text were refused in 1.0–7.1 % of their cases, always by a check that tripped over the changed wording — readability, or a drawing whose labels the shortened question no longer named — and never for the defect itself. Ten sampled cases of each out-of-scope operator were read one by one, by the AI assistant that ran the experiment, its verdicts to be checked by the author [ai-use-log; TBD: K04]: 4 of 10 questions turned to ask the opposite kept their answer or read as nothing, and 1 of 10 removed sentences held nothing the answer needed; the other sampled cases were the defects they are named after. A copy of a reference task with its people and objects renamed was refused as a near-duplicate in 95.5 % of 177 cases, and with its numbers changed as well in 88.2 % of 127: renaming leaves most of the text, so the textual check sees such a copy. The protocol had put it out of scope as the same idea in a new setting; read as a child reads it, it is a copy, and its refusal is the check working, while a plot written anew is a new task that the check lets through by design, as it does a paraphrase, which no operator here writes [ea1; Q78].

### 6.3 RQ2: simulated learners

**Design.** Simulated children go through the service's own rule, profile and update, with the shipped catalog, for 200 answers each; only the child is simulated. A child has a true level, drawn around the start of a random grade with a spread of one logit, and a true offset in every topic. The difficulty of a written task deviates from the one asked for by a normal error with a standard deviation of 0.5 logits, since a model does not hit a requested difficulty exactly. Nine generators of 1,000 children each take this population as it is or change it in one respect: children placed a grade level off, children who learn and forget, children whose level jumps by a logit at once, a host that writes harder tasks, topics linked in two groups, a slope or a guessing floor other than the model's, and hints. Fourteen alternatives run beside the service's update: its shrinking step with one level per child or one level per topic; a constant step and the likelihood-gradient step (the service's step weighted by w(P)), each with one level per child, one level per topic, or — the service's own structure — a level plus topic offsets; and Glicko-2 [@glickman2022glicko2], Glicko-2 with the guessing floor, and Urnings [@bolsinova2022urnings], which have no published form for a level split into two parts and run in the first two structures. Every rule meets the same children with the same random draws, and only the estimate differs: the choice of tasks, the trial's choice of topics and mastery are the service's for all of them. The step rules, like the service, keep the trial's placement through its five answers; Glicko-2 and Urnings estimate from the first answer, each moving fast at first in its own way, by a wide deviation or a small urn. The protocol was frozen and timestamped before the first run. Our Glicko-2 reproduces Glickman's worked example up to the rounding of his intermediate figures, and our Urnings the simulated example of its paper, at a smaller scale of 100 players and 2,000,000 games, to an average gap of 0.003 [ea3].

**Measures.** The error of the estimated level θ + δ_t over the topics a child has answered; the share of tasks whose true chance of success falls in the corridor; the share of false declarations of mastery, those made while the child's true chance on the level's middle task, difficulty 3 with no writing error, was below 0.775; the answers from true mastery to its declaration; how far the estimate lags behind a child who learns; and, for a child placed a level off, the error after five and ten answers and the failures among the first tasks. Four comparisons were named primary before the run: on children who stay put and on children who learn, the service against each alternative; on children placed a level off, the trial series against the update from the first answer; and floors under the step against none. Every number is a mean over the 1,000 children of its cell or, where a measure counts declarations, topics or attempts, a share pooled over them; each has a 95 % bootstrap interval over children, and a paired difference counts as found when its interval excludes zero; the rest is secondary [ea3].

**Results.** On children who stay put, the service's estimate and the gradient step in the same structure were the most accurate of the fifteen rules after 200 answers, with a root mean square error of 0.49 logits each and no difference found between them, against 0.58–0.83 for the other rules. The service's predicted chances were calibrated, with an expected calibration error of 0.004, and 42 % of its tasks fell in the corridor; only Glicko-2 with the floor, with one level per child, kept more, 43 %. Under every rule most tasks missed the corridor: it is 0.96 logits wide, the points of a level stand a logit apart, a task's writing error adds a spread of 0.5, and the estimate errs besides (Figure 2) [ea3]. Caption: The fifteen rules of the sweep on children who stay put: the share of tasks whose true chance of success fell in the corridor (left) and the share of declarations of mastery made while the child's true chance on the level's middle task was below 0.775 (right), each with its 95 % interval; the service is the filled mark.]

Mastery was the weak part. Of the service's declarations of mastery, 63 % came while the child's true chance on the level's middle task was below 0.775, and 53 % while it was below 0.70; under the other rules, which share the service's mastery rule, the share was 54–71 %: none of the fourteen other estimates made most declarations true. Of the children and topics not truly mastered at the level of their tasks, 44 % were declared mastered within their first five eligible attempts and 82 % within ten, by a Kaplan–Meier estimate that counts a topic whose attempts stopped early for those it made; on these attempts the child's true chance of success was 0.8 or more in 28 % and below 0.5 in 11 %. A topic the child truly mastered was declared a mean of 9.8 answers later, or, in 22 % of cases, not within the 200 answers [ea3].

Children who learn showed the cost of the shrinking step. With a level that grows by 0.01 logits an answer, two logits over the run, the service's estimate stood 1.12 logits below the truth on average over answers 101–200, and 26 % of its tasks fell in the corridor. A constant step in the same structure lagged by 0.48 and kept 38 % in the corridor; Glicko-2 and Urnings, with one level per child, lagged by 0.24 and 0.34. A constant step equal to the service's after 20 answers, overall and in a topic, as a secondary variant, lagged by 0.76 and was no less accurate on children who stay put, 0.48. After a jump of one logit, the service's error, averaged over the topics answered since, took a mean of 80 answers to settle within 0.5 logits of zero for ten answers running, a child who never settled counted at the run's end; for 71 % of children it never did. Under the constant step it took 37 answers, with 9 % unsettled. A floor of 0.05 under the level's step lowered that mean by 7 answers (95 % interval 6–8) and lowered the share never caught up with to 56 %, and cost nothing measurable on children who stay put. Floors of 0.01 and 0.02 left the catch-up unchanged and the error within a thousandth of a logit: the step falls below 0.02 only after 180 answers, and never below 0.01 within 200 [ea3].

For children placed a grade level off, the trial series did what it was built for. After five answers it stood a mean of 0.94 logits from the child's level, against 2.24 for the update from the first answer, and after ten 0.87 against 2.05; 23 % of the first ten tasks gave the child less than an even chance, against 39 %. The longest run of wrong answers among the first fifteen was two on average either way. Children whose grade placed them well paid for it: after five answers their overall level stood 0.87 logits from theirs with the trial and 0.72 without, and after 200 answers the root mean square error of their topic levels was still 0.49 against 0.47 [ea3].

Of the secondary variants, halving or doubling the first step made the estimate worse on children who stay put, 0.58 and 0.61; a corridor centred at 0.70 made it slightly better, 0.47, while 70 % of declarations of mastery were false; and one centred at 0.85 made it worse, 0.53, with mastery declared about once a child instead of ten times and 99 % of those declarations false. Under a slope or a floor other than the model's, the service's step and the gradient step settled together: 0.19 logits below the truth both for children with another slope, and 0.14 and 0.15 below for children with another floor [ea3].

### 6.4 Cost and latency

The service makes no model calls, so a task costs it only computation [C003]. On a development machine, one review at a time, each of the 447 reference tasks the checks accept was reviewed in ten passes. A review took a median of 3.45 ms (95 % interval over tasks 3.37–3.52; the median of a single pass came to 3.28–3.73 ms), 9.14 ms at the 95th percentile, 26.8 ms at the 99th and 63.2 ms at most. Most of it is judging, a median of 3.14 ms; reading the task and running its solver twice took a median of 0.18 ms. The solver run is the costly part when it is long, and its limits bound it [C024]: the slowest of the 8,940 runs took 35 ms, and the one with the most steps took 2.4 million, 9.7 % of the step limit. A review allocates 2.5 MiB on average. For a child with no interests and no notes, the package the model writes a task from weighed 17.2–25.2 KiB at every point of the ladder each topic is taught at, 175 in all, and at every turn of their reference tasks, at most 39 % of the 64 KiB budget a test holds every package to: about 5,000 tokens at the median and 6,200 at most, by the rough rule of four characters to a token; a parent's notes and the child's interests add their own length [ea4]. Measured on a development machine, in a container the size of one instance with the profile kept in memory, a task of a lesson is billed 0.36 vCPU-seconds and 0.36 GiB-seconds, which would let the platform's free tier cover about 500,000 tasks a month, and an instance asked for answers its first probe a median of 254 ms later; the round trips to Drive and the extra calls a host makes are not in these numbers [C086]. An instance lets in about three tasks handed in a second, the pace its limit of 200 messages a minute allows, and its one solver slot kept up with a costly solver at that pace [C101]. The runtime image weighed 6.8 MB when first deployed [C052]. What a task costs in time is the model's writing, which counts against the family's own use of the chat: in the first runs a task was accepted a median of 30 s after it was asked for, locally [C084], and 16–31 s later in Claude's web client [C085]. What the deployed service spends, and how many of a family's messages a task takes, are not yet measured [planned: T64].

### 6.5 Threats to validity

- **Internal.** The defects are injected by us, and may be easier to catch than the ones models make; the classes are fixed in advance, and the out-of-scope classes bound the claim from the other side. The checks reviewed reference tasks that our harness made into submissions, with the same filler texts in the fields a reference task lacks, not tasks a host's model handed in, and the review ran on its own, outside any conversation. The 120 out-of-scope cases were read by one reader, the AI assistant that ran the experiment; the author's check of its verdicts is still to come [TBD: K04]. The simulated children answer by the very curve the service assumes, a logistic one with the guessing floor, and the two generators that depart from it change only its slope or its floor (Section 6.3); this favours the service's update and the steps built on the same curve, and children whose answers follow another form might rank the rules otherwise.
- **External.** The reference tasks come from one model family (Section 3.3) and are written in English, and the readability limits were calibrated on them [P018]; tasks in that family's style may also make generation less diverse [P014], and how the checks fare in other languages is not measured. The simulated children are synthetic profiles drawn from a model of children, not children. The cost of a review was measured on one development machine; an instance of the service has one virtual processor [C005], and its times will differ.
- **Construct.** Catching an injected defect is not the same as catching a real one. The checks look for faults, not for a good task: whether a task is olympiad-style, or at the difficulty asked for, is asked for in the brief and not checked [Q72]. Readability measured by sentence length and Flesch–Kincaid is not comprehension.
- **Conclusion.** Some classes are small. Every share is reported with its interval, and the unit of analysis is the injected defect or the simulated child. Each of the simulation's 80 primary comparisons was judged by its own 95 % interval, with no correction for their number, and 65 found a difference; a few of these may be chance, most likely those whose interval barely leaves zero out, such as Glicko-2 with the floor keeping more tasks in the corridor than the service [ea3].
- **The search.** Our claims of novelty rest on searches in English and on the forward citations of the nearest works; the Russian tradition of olympiad problems was searched in Russian only through OpenAlex, whose results, judged by their titles, held methodological papers and programmes but no controlled evaluation and no generation of problems, and one systematic review of the generation of word problems could not be obtained [@utami2026systematic].

## 7 Limitations

- **No study with children.** Nothing here measures learning. In the trials of model tutoring we read, every gain came with correctness from outside the model [@kestin2025ai; @pardos2024chatgptgenerated; @learnlmteam2025ai], and even then not always [@bastani2025generative]; whether automated checks can take that place for a child is untested.
- **The checks see the formalisation, not the text.** A misreading that the solver and the key share passes (Section 4.5), and so does an ambiguity the model writes in [C089].
- **The model knows the answer.** Sealing protects the answer from the child, not from the model, from an adult who reads the host's own tool log, or from someone who inspects the card's memory [C064, C096].
- **The checks do not reach the conversation.** Instructions planted in data a model reads can steer it [@greshake2023not], and in a benchmark that connected an agent to a trusted server and a malicious one, the malicious server's tool descriptions often made it misuse the trusted server's tools [@wang2026mcptox; @beurerkellner2025mcp]. The parent's notes reach the chat's model with every task [C092]; the rule never reads them [C097], but they reach the model that talks to the child, and in text mode the model records the child's answer [C039].
- **Repeats are textual, and memory is bounded.** A new wording of a task the child has seen passes, and repeats are sought only among the last 200 tasks of the child and among the reference tasks of the task's level [C028, C029]. Injected copies were mostly refused — with people and objects renamed, or with new numbers — but short computations with new numbers often passed, and no paraphrase was tried [ea1].
- **The same answer in other words.** A second correct option, the right number written in words beside its digits, passed every check in every case we injected: options count as one answer only when they read alike or are the same number in digits [ea1].
- **The ladder's step between grade levels is a guess,** and so is the placement's prior spread, chosen by simulation; live runs are to check both [C010, C080].
- **Difficulty is asked for, not measured.** A task's β is the difficulty the brief requested, and the model may miss it (Section 6.3); the model may also choose a task outside the corridor itself [C083].
- **Most declarations of mastery are false, and a learner is followed slowly.** In simulation most declarations of mastery were made while the child's true chance on the level's middle task was below 0.775, and a child who learned steadily was left a logit behind, while a constant step lagged by half a logit (Section 6.3) [ea3]. A false declaration is undone by two wrong answers in a row, and a wrong answer brings an easier task in its topic [C015, C019], but a topic declared mastered leaves the rotation until every topic within reach is mastered [C070], so the correction waits for the topic to come back.
- **The sandbox does not price all memory.** A solver that grows a list by the language's own operators can exhaust an instance's memory [C075].
- **Two limits are reasoned, not measured:** sentence length in scripts written without spaces, and the bigram threshold for near-duplicates [C076].
- **Translations nobody has read.** Twenty of the 22 languages of the cards are machine translations [C087].
- **Text only.** Drawings are text [C031]; there are no images.

## 8 Discussion, Ethics and Conclusion

The design gives each party what it does well. The language model writes: a fresh problem for this child, in the child's language, in a setting the child likes. The service decides and checks: what to practise, whether the key is right, whether the task repeats one the child has seen. A task reaches the child's card only after the service has checked it; what the model writes into the chat itself, the checks do not reach (Section 7). Of its checks, only the self-check is the model's opinion of its own work; the solver is the model's program, but the service runs it and judges the result, though the program still shares the model's reading of the task (Section 4.5).

**Ethics.** No child took part in this study. The service learns no name, birth date or school: the child appears in the profile under a pseudonym and a random identifier [C069, C093]. A pseudonym does not make data anonymous — pseudonymised data remain personal data [@gdpr2016regulation; @mourby2018pseudonymised] — so the design keeps as little as it can instead: the child's profile lives in the parent's own Drive, reached with the parent's token, and the service keeps no copy [C002, C078]. Task texts, options, answers and the pseudonym stay out of logs and metrics, and tests hold them out [C036]; telemetry replaces attributes it may not keep with a fixed value [C037]. The service calls no model of its own, so it trains none [C003], and its privacy policy promises no advertising [C091]; the learner model serves only the choice of the next task and the family's own view of progress [C016, C018]. Some things do leave or stay. The child's grade and interests and the parent's free-form notes reach the chat's model with every task [C092]. And identifiers remain: the child's random one in the profile, a derived one for the parent's account on the service's log lines, and the address of each request in the platform's own logs [C093]. Neither host admits a child of primary age as a user: Claude's users must be at least 18 and ChatGPT's at least 13, with a parent's permission under 18, and for children under 13 in education ChatGPT asks that the adult conduct the interaction [@anthropic2025consumerterms; @openai2026terms; @openai2026safeallages]; its directory admits no app that explicitly targets children under 13 [@openai2026pluginguidelines]. MathTrail's terms make the adult the user, who runs the lesson; the child does not sign in and has no account [C098]. The card nonetheless lets the child answer and type a question into the chat in the child's own words [C039, C100]: there the child acts directly. We claim no compliance on a host's behalf. A study with children would need review by an ethics board and the parent's permission [G5], and, unless the board waives it, the child's own assent [@hhs2024subpartd; @bera2024ethical]; an experiment inside a lesson should compare sensible options only [@motz2018embedding].

**Conclusion.** Letting a chat model write for a child is safe only as far as what it writes is checked. We showed a service that admits a model's task only after a program the model wrote has confirmed the key twice, and that keeps the answer sealed until the child has answered. The evaluation measures what these checks catch and what they cannot [TBD: S63].
