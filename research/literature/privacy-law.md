## Who the law protects, and who counts as the user

**Keys:** `coppa2025rule`, `gdpr2016regulation`, `ico2020age`.

*Read:* the official texts, as the table says. The COPPA Rule and the code are cited by section; the GDPR by article or recital.

**The COPPA Rule** protects children under 13 and binds "operators": "Child means an individual under the age of 13." (§ 312.2). It applies to a service directed to children, or to an operator with actual knowledge: "It shall be unlawful for any operator of a website or online service directed to children, or any operator that has actual knowledge that it is collecting or maintaining personal information from a child, to collect personal information from a child in a manner that violates the regulations prescribed under this part." (§ 312.3). Whether a service is directed to children is judged by many factors: "the Commission will consider its subject matter, visual content, use of animated characters or child-oriented activities and incentives, music or other audio content, age of models, presence of child celebrities or celebrities who appeal to children, language or other characteristics of the website or online service" (§ 312.2, "Website or online service directed to children", paragraph (1)). The definition of an operator covers services "operated for commercial purposes" and excludes "any nonprofit entity that would otherwise be exempt from coverage under Section 5 of the Federal Trade Commission Act" (§ 312.2, "Operator").

**The GDPR** asks for a parent's consent when consent is the basis of processing for a service offered directly to a child: "Where point (a) of Article 6(1) applies, in relation to the offer of information society services directly to a child, the processing of the personal data of a child shall be lawful where the child is at least 16 years old. Where the child is below the age of 16 years, such processing shall be lawful only if and to the extent that consent is given or authorised by the holder of parental responsibility over the child." Member states may lower the age "provided that such lower age is not below 13 years", and "The controller shall make reasonable efforts to verify in such cases that consent is given or authorised by the holder of parental responsibility over the child, taking into consideration available technology." (Article 8(1)–(2)). Its reason: "Children merit specific protection with regard to their personal data, as they may be less aware of the risks, consequences and safeguards concerned and their rights in relation to the processing of personal data." (Recital 38).

**The Age Appropriate Design Code** reaches further than both. A child is anyone under 18, and the code covers services a child is likely to use, not only services aimed at children: "This code applies to “information society services likely to be accessed by children” in the UK. This includes many apps, programs, connected toys and devices, search engines, social media platforms, streaming services, online games, news or educational websites and websites offering other goods or services to users over the internet. It is not restricted to services specifically directed at children." ("Services covered by this code", at a glance). "Likely" means "more probable than not" (same page). Free services are not outside it: "This code also covers not-for-profit apps, games and educational sites, as long as those services can be considered as ‘economic activity’ in a more general sense." (same page).

**What this means for MathTrail.** The parent signs in, and the child uses the card inside the parent's chat. Under COPPA that makes the question whether the service is directed to children or knows it collects from one — a maths app for grades 1–6 has the subject matter, if not the look, the factors name. Under the GDPR the question is whether the service is offered directly to a child. Under the code, whether a child is more likely than not to use it — which a lesson for a child is by design. None of the three turns on who holds the account. Paper A therefore does not argue that the law does not apply because an adult signs in; it describes what the design collects and keeps.

## What may be collected, and how

**Keys:** `coppa2025rule`, `ftc2025childrens`, `gdpr2016regulation`, `ico2020age`, `mourby2018pseudonymised`.

**Personal information is wide.** Under COPPA it includes "A persistent identifier that can be used to recognize a user over time and across different websites or online services. Such persistent identifier includes, but is not limited to, a customer number held in a cookie, an Internet Protocol (IP) address, a processor or device serial number, or unique device identifier" and "Information concerning the child or the parents of that child that the operator collects online from the child and combines with an identifier described in this definition." (§ 312.2, "Personal information", paragraphs (7) and (11)). One exception bears on identifiers an operator keeps to run the service: no prior parental consent is needed "Where an operator collects a persistent identifier and no other personal information and such identifier is used for the sole purpose of providing support for the internal operations of the website or online service. In such case, the operator shall provide notice under § 312.4(d)(3)" (§ 312.5(c)(7)); internal operations include activities necessary to "Maintain or analyze the functioning of the website or online service" and to "Protect the security or integrity of the user, website, or online service" (§ 312.2, "Support for the internal operations of the website or online service").

**A pseudonym is not anonymity.** The GDPR defines pseudonymisation as processing "in such a manner that the personal data can no longer be attributed to a specific data subject without the use of additional information, provided that such additional information is kept separately and is subject to technical and organisational measures" (Article 4(5)), and says of the result: "Personal data which have undergone pseudonymisation, which could be attributed to a natural person by the use of additional information should be considered to be information on an identifiable natural person." (Recital 26). Mourby et al. read the two together: "The definition of pseudonymisation under the GDPR is not intended to determine whether data are personal data; indeed it is clear that all data falling within this definition are personal data. Rather, it is Recital 26 and its requirement of a ‘means reasonably likely to be used’ which remains the relevant test as to whether data are personal." (abstract, p. 222). So a profile keyed by a pseudonym, in a parent's Drive, is personal data about a child.

**Minimise, by design and by default.** Personal data must be "adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed (‘data minimisation’)" (Article 5(1)(c)). The controller "shall, both at the time of the determination of the means for processing and at the time of the processing itself, implement appropriate technical and organisational measures, such as pseudonymisation, which are designed to implement data-protection principles, such as data minimisation, in an effective manner" (Article 25(1)), and "shall implement appropriate technical and organisational measures for ensuring that, by default, only personal data which are necessary for each specific purpose of the processing are processed. That obligation applies to the amount of personal data collected, the extent of their processing, the period of their storage and their accessibility." (Article 25(2)). The code's standard 8 says the same for children: "Collect and retain only the minimum amount of personal data you need to provide the elements of your service in which a child is actively and knowingly engaged." ("8. Data minimisation").

**Consent to disclosure, and AI training.** Since 2025 a COPPA operator must let a parent consent to collection without consenting to disclosure: "An operator must give the parent the option to consent to the collection and use of the child's personal information without consenting to disclosure of his or her personal information to third parties, unless such disclosure is integral to the website or online service. An operator required to give the parent this option must obtain separate verifiable parental consent to such disclosure." (§ 312.5(a)(2)). The FTC's statement names what is not integral: "Disclosures of a child's personal information to third parties for monetary or other consideration, for advertising purposes, or to train or otherwise develop artificial intelligence technologies, are not integral to the website or online service and would require consent pursuant to the proposed amendments to Sec. 312.5(a)(2)." (90 FR 16950). The amended rule took effect 60 days after publication, with "365 days from the final Rule's publication date to come into full compliance with the amendments that do not specify earlier compliance dates" (90 FR 16971) — that is, by 22 April 2026.

**Retention.** "Personal information collected online from a child may not be retained indefinitely. At a minimum, the operator must establish, implement, and maintain a written data retention policy that sets forth the purposes for which children's personal information is collected, the business need for retaining such information, and a timeframe for deletion of such information." (§ 312.10).

**Profiling.** The GDPR's profiling is "any form of automated processing of personal data consisting of the use of personal data to evaluate certain personal aspects relating to a natural person, in particular to analyse or predict aspects concerning that natural person's performance at work, economic situation, health, personal preferences, interests, reliability, behaviour, location or movements" (Article 4(4)). The code's standard 12 asks that profiling be off by default, "unless you can demonstrate a compelling reason for profiling to be on by default, taking account of the best interests of the child" ("12. Profiling"), and makes one exception: "There is no point in offering a privacy setting if the profiling is essential to the provision of the core service that the child has requested. This is because if the profiling were turned off there would be no residual service left for the child to use. This concept should be interpreted narrowly, eg that it is completely intrinsic to the service." (same page).

**What paper A takes.** MathTrail's learner model estimates how well a child solves tasks, which is an evaluation of a personal aspect, and its choice of the next task rests on it: it may well be profiling in the GDPR's sense. It is also what the service is — adaptive practice with nothing left without it — which is the case the code's exception describes. Paper A says that the ratings serve only the choice of the next task and the family's own view of progress (C018, C016), and does not call the child's data anonymous: it is pseudonymised personal data, kept in the parent's own storage (C069, C078).

## What aggregate numbers protect, and what they do not

**Keys:** `sweeney2002kanonymity`, `dwork2014algorithmic`, `desfontaines2019cardinality`, `ramage2020federated`, `kairouz2021advances`.

**k-anonymity.** *Read:* the abstract, the introduction and the definitions, from the author's copy, whose header gives the journal's pages 557–570.
- The definition: "A release provides k-anonymity protection if the information for each person contained in the release cannot be distinguished from at least k-1 individuals whose information also appears in the release." (abstract, p. 557).
- Why it was needed: a US study found "87% (216 million of 248 million) of the population in the United States had reported characteristics that likely made them unique based only on {5-digit ZIP, gender, date of birth}" (p. 558). A few coarse attributes identify people.
- Its own limit: "This paper also examines re-identification attacks that can be realized on releases that adhere to k-anonymity unless accompanying policies are respected." (abstract, p. 557).

**Differential privacy.** *Read:* chapter 1, "The Promise of Differential Privacy", and chapter 2, from the authors' copy; the pages are the copy's.
- The promise: "“Differential privacy” describes a promise, made by a data holder, or curator, to a data subject: “You will not be affected, adversely or otherwise, by allowing your data to be used in any study or analysis, no matter what other studies, data sets, or information sources, are available.”" (p. 5).
- The limit every release meets: "data utility will eventually be consumed: the Fundamental Law of Information Recovery states that overly accurate answers to too many questions will destroy privacy in a spectacular way." (p. 5).
- The definition, its formula transcribed from the PDF: a randomized algorithm M "is (ε, δ)-differentially private if for all S ⊆ Range(M) and for all x, y ∈ ℕ^|X| such that ‖x − y‖₁ ≤ 1: Pr[M(x) ∈ S] ≤ exp(ε) Pr[M(y) ∈ S] + δ" (Definition 2.4, p. 17). A mechanism is private or not by this property of its output distribution, not by the size of the cells it publishes.

**Sketches are not anonymous.** *Read:* the abstract and introduction, the published version. "We conclude that strong aggregation requirements are incompatible with any reasonable definition of privacy, and that cardinality estimators should be considered as sensitive as raw data." (abstract, p. 26). The product's plan for usage evidence rejected HyperLogLog sketches on this paper's ground (`docs/usage-evidence-for-grants.md`, §4.2).

**Federated analytics.** *Read:* the post in full; the survey's abstract and its passages on federated analytics.
- The practice as its authors named it: "Today we’re introducing federated analytics, the practice of applying data science methods to the analysis of raw data that is stored locally on users’ devices. Like federated learning, it works by running local computations over each device’s data, and only making the aggregated results — and never any data from a particular device — available to product engineers." (Ramage and Mazzocchi 2020, paragraph 2).
- The survey that places it beside federated learning: "FL embodies the principles of focused data collection and minimization, and can mitigate many of the systemic privacy risks and costs resulting from traditional, centralized machine learning and data science approaches." (Kairouz et al., abstract); it names "federated analytics tasks like model evaluation and debugging" as components beside training (section 1.1.1 of arXiv version 3).

**What paper A takes, and how MathTrail differs.**
- MathTrail keeps its data where federated analytics keeps it — with its owner, in the parent's own Drive (C078) — but not by federated means. Each request brings the whole profile to the service, which computes on it in memory and writes it back. What leaves it is the package for the chat's model, which carries the child's grade and interests and the parent's notes (C092), the task card, and log lines about events built from named fields (C035, C036). The principle is shared, the mechanism is not, and paper A says "in the spirit of", not "federated".
- The product's plan for public usage numbers — one dimension per table, cells of at least 10, rounding to 5 (`docs/usage-evidence-for-grants.md`, §4.4; planned in T67) — is a threshold rule of the k-anonymity kind. It is not differential privacy, and by the fundamental law many such tables over time can still reveal what the thresholds hide. That plan is not built and paper A reports no usage numbers; the point goes to the author as a remark for T67 (Remarks).
- The MinHash sketches the profile keeps of past tasks (C029) are sketches of task text, not of children, and they stay in the parent's file. By Desfontaines et al.'s conclusion they are as sensitive as the text they summarise; paper A describes them as a way to avoid storing text, not as anonymisation.

## Studies with children, and experiments inside a lesson

**Keys:** `hhs2024subpartd`, `bera2024ethical`, `gdpr2016regulation`, `motz2018embedding`.

**Permission and assent.** US federal rules for research with children, which bind research the Department of Health and Human Services conducts or supports (§ 46.401(b)) and which many ethics boards use as their model, define the two agreements a study needs: "Assent means a child's affirmative agreement to participate in research. Mere failure to object should not, absent affirmative agreement, be construed as assent." and "Permission means the agreement of parent(s) or guardian to the participation of their child or ward in research." (§ 46.402(b)–(c)). Research of no more than minimal risk is approved "only if the IRB finds that adequate provisions are made for soliciting the assent of the children and the permission of their parents or guardians" (§ 46.404). Assent is sought where the board judges the children capable of it, and a board may waive either requirement. Assent: where the children cannot reasonably be consulted, where the research offers a direct benefit available only within it, and — "Even where the IRB determines that the subjects are capable of assenting" — wherever consent may be waived under § 46.116 (§ 46.408(a)). Permission: on the same grounds of § 46.116, or where it "is not a reasonable requirement to protect the subjects (for example, neglected or abused children)", provided another mechanism protects them (§ 46.408(c)). And some research is exempt altogether, including with children (§ 46.401(c)(2)): "Research, conducted in established or commonly accepted educational settings, that specifically involves normal educational practices that are not likely to adversely impact students' opportunity to learn required educational content or the assessment of educators who provide instruction." (§ 46.104(d)(1)). Whether a chat at home is such a setting is for a board to judge.

BERA asks the same of educational research in the UK: "Principles of consent apply to children and young people as well as to adults. This is termed assent for those under the age of legal consent in the setting/culture." It adds the child's best interests: "BERA endorses the United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child (UNCRC); the best interests of the child are the primary consideration, and children who are capable of forming their own views should be granted the right to express those views freely, and have them taken into consideration, in all matters affecting them, commensurate with their age and maturity." (paragraph 23, pp. 17–18). And consent is sought from those responsible for the child: "This may involve gaining the consent of those responsible for children, such as parents, guardians or others in loco parentis." (paragraph 24, p. 18).

**Research under the GDPR.** Research on personal data needs safeguards that "ensure that technical and organisational measures are in place in particular in order to ensure respect for the principle of data minimisation. Those measures may include pseudonymisation provided that those purposes can be fulfilled in that manner. Where those purposes can be fulfilled by further processing which does not permit or no longer permits the identification of data subjects, those purposes shall be fulfilled in that manner." (Article 89(1)).

**Experiments inside a lesson.** *Read:* in full, the published version; the journal's pages 47–59.
- The question an A/B test in education raises: "The notion of intentionally manipulating a learner’s educational experience for the purpose of research raises an important ethical question: What if condition B is reliably inferior to condition A? Has the research harmed the learners in this case?" (p. 52).
- Their answer compares it with what happens anyway: "Thus, unbeknownst to them, students in practically all classrooms are participants in a vast enterprise of uncontrolled experimentation." (p. 52).
- And sets a floor: "At the very least, an embedded experiment should contrast sensible design options, and should not administer a treatment known to or believed to potentially cause decrements in learning outcomes." (p. 52). A crossover design, in which every group gets every treatment at a different time, avoids any group bearing the risk alone (p. 52).
- The paper discusses university courses and does not take up consent; it does not speak to experiments on young children.

**What paper A takes.** No child takes part in paper A's study (G5). A future study of MathTrail with children needs an ethics board, which decides whether it is exempt and whether assent or permission may be waived; by default it asks for the parent's permission and the child's own assent. And it should compare sensible options only — never withhold what is known to help. Comparing two versions of the rule, say, is the kind of embedded experiment Motz et al. describe, and it would inherit their floor.

## The area's answers

1. **Who is protected.** COPPA protects children under 13, the GDPR's article 8 children under 16 (or down to 13 by national law) where consent is the basis, and the UK code everyone under 18 on a service a child is likely to use. None of the tests turns on who holds the account, so the parent's sign-in does not take MathTrail out of their scope; whether and how each applies is for a lawyer.
2. **What may be kept.** As little as the purpose needs, by design and by default (GDPR articles 5 and 25, the code's standard 8), for no longer than needed (COPPA § 312.10). A pseudonym reduces risk and does not make data anonymous (Recital 26). Disclosure to third parties for advertising or AI training needs a separate consent under COPPA since 2025. MathTrail's design keeps the child's data in the parent's storage, keeps no copy, logs no content and discloses nothing for training or advertising (C002, C036, C078, C091). It does keep identifiers — a random one for the child in the profile, a derived one for the parent's account in its logs, the requester's address in the platform's logs (C093) — and it sends the child's grade and interests and the parent's notes to the chat provider with each task (C092). The privacy policy names the notes but neither the grade and interests nor the logged identifier (C094).
3. **What aggregates protect.** A cell of at least k is k-anonymity, which linkage and repeated releases can defeat; only a mechanism like differential privacy gives a guarantee that survives other releases, and even it is used up by too many accurate answers. Sketches are as sensitive as their data. MathTrail reports no aggregates in paper A.
4. **What a study with children needs.** An ethics board, which decides whether the study is exempt and whether assent or permission may be waived; by default the parent's permission and the child's assent; safeguards for the data; and — for experiments inside a lesson — only sensible comparisons, none known to harm learning.
5. **What paper A says.** Its ethics paragraph states that no child took part; that the service keeps pseudonymised personal data in the parent's own storage rather than anonymous data in its own; that it logs no content, trains nothing and shows no advertising; that the child's grade and interests and the parent's notes reach the chat's model with every task; that identifiers remain — for the child in the profile, for the parent's account in the logs, the requester's address in the platform's logs; that the learner model serves only the choice of the next task; and that a study with children would need an ethics board, parental permission and the child's assent. It claims no compliance with any law, and it states the privacy policy's promises only as promises (C091).

## Remarks

- **Not legal advice.** Whether COPPA's definition of an operator reaches a free, open-source service run by an individual, whether MathTrail is "offered directly to a child" under the GDPR, and whether the parent's notes going to the chat provider are integral disclosures, are questions for a lawyer. The notes do not answer them and paper A does not claim an answer.
- **For the author, not critical (G6): what the privacy policy leaves out.** It names the notes as what reaches the chat's model, not the grade and the interests the package also carries, and it says the logs hold counts without naming the derived identifier of the parent's account on every signed-in line (C094). Recorded in Q66.
- **For the author, not critical (G6): the public usage numbers of T67.** The planned rule — cells of at least 10, rounding to 5, one dimension per table — protects each table on its own. Differences between tables released over months can still single out a family, which is the fundamental law's point; differential privacy, or releasing fewer and coarser tables, is the textbook answer. The research changes nothing in the product; this is recorded as Q66.
- **Left out.** The 2023 IDC paper on what the UK code means for designers (Grace, Abel and Salen) could not be read: both the ACM copy and the eScholarship copy refused automated readers. Livingstone and Third's agenda of children's digital rights was fetched but not needed for any claim here. Russia's law on personal data (152-FZ), which the prototype's note named, is outside the venues paper A targets.
- **Access.** EUR-Lex answers automated readers with a challenge; the GDPR was read from the Publications Office's own copy of the Official Journal, which carries the same text.
