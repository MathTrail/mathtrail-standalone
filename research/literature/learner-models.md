# Learner models, target success and mastery

The notes of task S32 (`protocol.md`, section 1). The questions:
- where MathTrail's response model comes from: Rasch, and the three-parameter model with a guessing floor;
- Elo in adaptive practice: the update, how its step is chosen, and what a step that only shrinks costs;
- what Glicko, Glicko-2, TrueSkill and Urnings add, and whether any of them handles a guessing floor;
- models that join a general level with topic levels, and whether MathTrail's θ + δ_topic is one of them;
- knowledge tracing (BKT, PFA, DKT), and why it does not fit tasks written once for one child;
- mastery criteria, their errors and trade-offs;
- the target success rate: the 85 % rule and the experiments on it;
- stochastic approximation, the convergence result behind a shrinking step;
- the case against a language model as the learner model.

## 1. The response model: Rasch and the guessing floor

**Keys:** `rasch1961general`, `birnbaum1968some`.

**Rasch (1961)** summarises the models of his 1960 book and the property that defines them: "The models have a remarkable property in common that renders it possible, in the analysis of the data, to detach the personal parameters from the stimulus parameters, and vice versa." (p. 321). Item parameters are estimated with persons replaced by their scores: "On the basis of (3.10) we may estimate the item parameters independently of the personal parameters, the latter having been replaced by something observable, namely, by the individual total number of correct answers." (p. 325).

**Birnbaum (1968)** defines the three-parameter models with a lower asymptote.
- The reason: "Even subjects of very low ability will sometimes give correct responses to multiple-choice items, just by chance." (p. 404). The assumption: "it further assumes that if he does not know it he will guess, and, with probability Cg, will guess correctly." (p. 404).
- The logistic form: "Similarly with the logistic model, we may take account of guessing probabilities by using modified item characteristic curves" (p. 405). His example uses five alternatives (p. 404), and Rasch's model is the restricted case: "Rasch (1960) has developed the test model of this restricted logistic form." (p. 402).
- The floor is an item parameter, and its form does not rest on the guessing story: "The psychological hypothesis implicit here has been mentioned primarily to point up a mathematical feature of this form; the empirical validity of this form is not dependent on this psychological hypothesis." (p. 404).

**What paper A takes.** MathTrail's curve, P = 0.2 + 0.8σ(θ + δ − β) [C009], is Birnbaum's logistic three-parameter model with the slope fixed at one, as Rasch's is, and the floor fixed at one in five, as in Birnbaum's five-option illustration; the Elo literature ties the same form to the three-parameter model (section 2). It takes Rasch's form, not his estimation: separating person and item parameters needs many persons on the same items, and MathTrail's tasks are used once, so β is declared, not calibrated [C010, C013]. The fixed 0.2 is an assumption: MathTrail's wrong options are traps, which may draw a child who does not know below one in five, while ruling options out may lift a child above it.

## 2. Elo in adaptive practice

**Keys:** `pelanek2016applications`, `klinkenberg2011computer`, `papousek2014adaptive`, `niznan2015student`, `pelanek2017elobased`.

**Pelánek (2016)** surveys Elo for education.
- Elo and Rasch: "In this formulation the Elo rating system is closely related to the Rasch model (one parameter model) used in IRT. These two approaches share the same functional form of the model for predicting the probability of a correct answer, they differ only in the parameter estimation procedure." (PDF p. 5).
- The step: "If K is small, the estimation converges too slowly, if K is large, the estimation is unstable as it gives too large a weighting to the last few attempts" (PDF p. 6). A shrinking one: "Papoušek et al. (2014) and Nižnan et al. (2015) use an uncertainty function U(n) = a/(1 + bn), where n is the number of answered questions and a, b are meta-parameters fitted to data (a = 1, b = 0.05)" (PDF p. 7). When it suits: "If the skill is expected to change quickly, K should stay large. If the skill is expected to be (nearly) constant, we can use an uncertainty function which approaches zero with an increasing number of attempts" (PDF p. 8).
- The recommendation: "the basic version of the Elo rating system extended with a simple uncertainty function U(n) = a/(1 + bn) provides a good starting point (e.g., with values a = 1, b = 0.05; once enough data are collected these values can be easily fitted using a grid search)." (PDF p. 13).
- Bayesian trackers resist the floor: "The approach used by Glicko and TrueSkill is, however, difficult to modify for new situations, e.g., when we want to use the shifted logistic function for modeling answers to multiple-choice questions." (PDF p. 7).

**Math Garden** (Klinkenberg et al.): children practise arithmetic, and the child's and the item's ratings both move after every answer.
- The target: "Items are sampled with a mean success probability of .75, making the tasks challenging yet not too difficult." (thesis p. 24). Drawn per item: "where probability P is randomly drawn from a normal distribution P ∼ N(.75,.1) and restricted such that .5 < P < 1." (p. 32). What children met: "The graphs show that the proportion of correctly answered items varied between .6 and .8 for most children." (p. 39).
- Uncertainty falls with use and grows with absence: "Therefore uncertainty reduces to zero after 40 administrations and conversely increases to the maximum of 1 after 30 days D of not playing." (p. 30). Answers are scored for speed and accuracy, not right or wrong alone.

**Where the shrinking step comes from** (Papoušek et al. 2014): "instead of the constant K we use an uncertainty function a 1+bn, where n is the order of the answer and a, b are parameters. Using a grid search we have determined optimal values a = 1, b = 0.05. This exact choice of parameter values is not important, many different choices of a, b provide very similar results." (PDF p. 3; "a 1+bn" is the fraction a/(1+bn) as extracted). It was fitted on the first answers of a geography system. A warning that applies to any adaptive rule: "For example, if the model overestimates the knowledge of students, the question selection stops asking questions about items too early, which means that the system does not collect data that would contradict the overestimated knowledge." (PDF p. 7).

**A global level corrected by a concept level** (Nižnan et al. 2015; Pelánek et al. 2017).
- "In addition to the global skill θs the model now uses also the concept skill θsc." "we just correct the global skill by the concept skill: P(correct|θs, θsc, di) = σ((θs + θsc) − di)." (Nižnan et al., p. 111). The concept's update is scaled: "γ is a meta-parameter specifying sensitivity of the model to concepts" (p. 111). Their step: "We chose α = 1 and β = 0.06 and we used these values also for derived models which use the uncertainty function." (p. 112).
- The journal version: "We use an uncertainty function U (n) = α/(1 + βn), where n is the number of previous updates to the estimated parameter and α, β are meta-parameters." (Pelánek et al., PDF p. 8). The floor: "For a multiple choice question the probability of a correct answer is the combination of the probability of guessing the answer (Pguess ) and knowing the target item (Pest ): Psuccess = Pguess + (1 − Pguess ) · Pest ." (PDF p. 21), "The same approach to modeling guessing is used for example, in the standard three-parameter logistic model of item response theory (De Ayala, 2008)." (PDF p. 6).
- Item difficulty is learned from many learners: "di := di + K · (P (correct|θl , di ) − correct)," (PDF p. 8). And a limit: "For more complex knowledge components (e.g., rules) and domains with more involved structure (e.g, prerequisites among knowledge components), the basic Elo rating system is probably not sufficient." (PDF p. 27).

**What paper A takes.** MathTrail's learner model is this hierarchical Elo of adaptive practice: a general level θ corrected by a topic level δ, a floor for five options, and steps K₀/(1 + 0.05n) counted separately for θ and for each topic, the topic's starting at twice the general one [C009, C011]. It differs in three ways, each with a reason: its β is set by design on one ladder, not learned, because no task is used twice [C010, C013]; its K₀ is 0.2 and 0.4 against 1; and its b is Papoušek et al.'s 0.05, not Nižnan et al.'s 0.06 (Pelánek 2016 attributes 0.05 to both). These systems taught geography facts and arithmetic fluency; olympiad problems are the kind of domain Pelánek et al. call too complex for basic Elo, which is a limit paper A should state. MathTrail scores accuracy only [C012], with no time, and its target of 0.775 lies inside Math Garden's draw around .75, two and a half points above its mean.

## 3. The step: what a shrinking K costs

**Keys:** `vermeiren2025balancing`, `bolsinova2025keeping`.

**Vermeiren et al. (2025)** compare schemes for K and propose one that follows the trend of the residuals.
- "Existing modifications of the Elo system, which diminish K as the number of responses increases, are inadequate in scenarios characterized by considerable ability fluctuation, a common occurrence in digital learning environments." (abstract).
- The trade-off: "It is evident that when ability remains constant and ratings converge, implementation of a decreasing K function results in more stable estimates." and "However, when halfway through the simulation a jump in ability is introduced, the decreasing K function takes notably more time to converge due to the small K values at this point." ("Balance of accuracy and convergence"). For a learner who grows steadily: "However, since the learner continues to get better (i.e., a gradual increase in ability levels) the K value will become too small to keep up with the increasing ability levels."
- Math Garden today: "In a learning environment, the goal is to motivate learners to keep practicing their skills; therefore, often a success rate of .75 is aimed for." "Specifically, they can choose between three levels: easy (about 90% correct), medium (about 75% correct), or hard (about 60% correct)." And: "For persons, a minimum K value of .2 is implemented" ("Math garden data").
- Their remedy does not carry over as it stands: "Note that the rationale of alternating between positive and negative updates does not hold up for adaptivity rules higher than .5 when only accuracy data are taken into account." ("Math garden data").

**Bolsinova et al. (2025)** simulate Elo with items fixed or updated, chosen at random or adaptively.
- "Our results show that the Elo ratings are generally not unbiased and their variances are context‐dependent." (abstract).
- The case of fixed items chosen adaptively: "(1) In the random scenarios, the Elo ratings have outward bias, while in the fixed‐adaptive scenario, bias is negative for p>.5." (§5). It grows with K and with the target's distance from .5: "For p=.5 there is hardly any bias, and the magnitude of bias increases when P moves away from .5." (§2.2).
- Aiming high costs answers: "Increasing p from .7 to .9 results in roughly 50% more responses needed to converge, which is similar to decreasing K from .3 to .2." (§2.2).

**What paper A takes.** MathTrail's step belongs to the shrinking family that lags behind a learner who changes: by our arithmetic θ's step halves after 20 answers and falls to a sixth after 100, and it never grows back [C011]. Math Garden now puts a floor of .2 under a learner's K; MathTrail has none, and the draft already names that as v1's choice. MathTrail is Bolsinova et al.'s fixed-adaptive case — β fixed, a target above .5 — so by analogy its levels are biased low; their simulations had a constant K, no floor in the curve and no learning, so S38 measures this rather than citing it as a number.

## 4. Bayesian trackers: Glicko, Glicko-2, TrueSkill, Urnings

**Keys:** `glickman1999parameter`, `glickman2016glicko`, `glickman2001dynamic`, `glickman2022glicko2`, `herbrich2007trueskill`, `bolsinova2022urnings`, `hofman2020tracking`.

- **Glicko** gives the step its meaning, the uncertainty of the rating: "The Elo updating algorithm is seen to be a special case of equation (9)." "Thus, in contrast with the Elo updating formula, the value of K depends on the prior precision of one's strength." (Glickman 1999, p. 382). Uncertainty grows while a player is away: "This model asserts that, as time passes while a player is not competing, the description of a competitor's strength becomes more uncertain." (p. 379). The author's own warning about a step that only shrinks: "One practical problem with the Glicko system is that when a player competes very frequently, his/her rating stops changing appreciably which reflects that the RD is very small." and the cure: "I would therefore recommend that an RD never drop below a threshold value, such as 30, so that ratings can change appreciably even in a relatively short time." (Glickman 2016, PDF p. 6).
- **Glicko-2** lets uncertainty grow after surprises: "However, under the stochastic variance model, the variance may increase after data are observed." (Glickman 2001, p. 688); "The volatility measure indicates the degree of expected fluctuation in a player’s rating." (Glickman 2022, p. 1). It assumes rating periods: "The Glicko-2 system works best when the number of games in a rating period is moderate to large, say an average of at least 10-15 games per player in a rating period." (p. 1).
- **TrueSkill** tracks Gaussian beliefs by message passing: "The new system tracks the uncertainty about player skills, explicitly models draws, can deal with any number of competing entities and can infer individual skills from team results." (abstract); "As can be seen, TrueSkill automatically chooses the correct learning rate whereas Elo only slowly converges to the target skill." (§4.1).
- **Urnings** give known standard errors and correct for adaptive selection: "A unique feature that we have not yet seen in other rating systems is that in our rating update we explicitly take the adaptive matchmaking of players into account" (Bolsinova et al. 2022, p. 110), at a price: "The good statistical properties of our rating system come at the price of ignoring some observations." (p. 110). "In our rating system, the urn sizes (n) determines how fast ratings are allowed to change." (p. 110). Elo's variance inflates under adaptive selection: "However, in all situations where items are adapted to the ability of players some inflation of rating variance occurs." (Hofman et al., §1.4).
- **MV-Glicko**, from its abstract only: a Glicko-based model for learners that "is sensitive to the lag time between two consecutive interactions of a student within the system and models it as a parameter that captures the confidence of the system in the current inferred rating" (Abdi et al. 2021). The abstract does not say whether it models guessing.

**What paper A takes.** These trackers give a principled size to the step, and two of them let the step grow back, through time away or surprise. None of the four read in full, as published, has a guessing floor: their outcome models are Bradley–Terry, a Gaussian difference or the urn game, and Pelánek reports that Glicko and TrueSkill are hard to adapt to one (section 2). The one Glicko variant built for learners that the searches found, MV-Glicko, was read from its abstract only, which is silent on guessing. Urnings would discard some of the few answers a child gives, and assume items that many learners update. Paper A cites them as the alternatives it did not take, and why.

## 5. A general level plus topic levels

**Keys:** `park2019multidimensional`, `vermeiren2025evaluating`, `rijmen2009three`, and, from their abstracts only, `adams1997multidimensional` and `wang2005rasch`.

- **Multidimensional Elo with a primary dimension** (Park et al. 2019): "Item bank 1 consists of a primary dimension θ1 indicated by all the items, and two auxiliary dimensions, θ2 and θ3, indicated by 35% of items and the 25% of the remaining items." Item difficulty is fixed: "Note that we assume that item difficulty parameters are considered as known (based on a calibration study), and the difficulty estimates therefore will not be further updated within the M-ERS." The auxiliary dimensions settle slowly: "In these two auxiliary dimensions, however, the MSE does not reach 0.2, even up to 200 items." ("Study 2").
- **MERS against MELO** (Vermeiren et al., EDM 2025): "Our results demonstrate that both multidimensional extensions outperform the unidimensional Elo rating system when the underlying data is multidimensional" (abstract); "In the MERS a weight of 1 is used for all skills involved, while in the MELO the weights of all the skills involved sum to 1." (§1). Their limits: "Furthermore, to keep the simulation design simple, we only looked at random item selection." (§5).
- **The psychometric family** (Rijmen 2009): "In the bifactor model, each item is an indicator of a general dimension and one of K other dimensions." (p. 1); "The testlet model (Bradlow, Wainer, & Wang, 1999) is a bifactor model in which the loadings on the specific dimensions are restricted to be proportional to the loadings on the general dimension within each testlet (Li, Bolt, & Fu, 2006)." and "The second-order model is formally equivalent to the testlet model." (p. i). A one-parameter version and a guessing parameter are allowed: "a “one parameter” bifactor model is obtained. Alternatively, an item guessing parameter can also be incorporated" (p. 2). The specific dimensions are set apart from the general one: "This can be achieved by setting the correlations between each of the K specific dimensions and the general dimension to zero." (p. 3).
- From the abstracts: the multidimensional random coefficients multinomial logit model "is presented as an extension to the Adams and Wilson (1996) random coefficients multinomial logit model" (Adams et al. 1997), and the Rasch testlet model "can be viewed as a special case of the multidimensional random coefficients multinomial logit model (MRCMLM)." (Wang and Wilson 2005).

**What paper A takes.** As a measurement model, θ + δ_topic − β with unit loadings and a fixed floor is a one-parameter bifactor model with a guessing parameter; a general level with topic levels, a bifactor structure, is the honest label, and a testlet model is a formal match only, since MathTrail's topics are content areas, not bundles sharing a stimulus. Two differences matter. In these models the topic dimensions are random effects estimated over a population and kept apart from the general one; MathTrail has no population, and within a topic only the sum θ + δ is identified, its split following from the steps, as the draft's Section 5.2 says. And the topic levels converge slowly in every simulation read, so a child's δ stays uncertain for many tasks. In Vermeiren et al.'s terms the structure is within-item: "Within-item dimensionality refers to the case where an item requires multiple abilities to be solved." (§1), and every task loads on θ and on its topic. The plan names multidimensional Rasch models between items (Adams et al.); the works read do not define a between-item structure, and Adams et al.'s abstract does not use the term, so the notes claim nothing about it beyond this.

## 6. Knowledge tracing

**Keys:** `corbett1995knowledge`, `pavlik2009performance`, `piech2015deep`.

- **BKT** (Corbett and Anderson): "Knowledge tracing assumes a two-state learning model. Each coding rule is either in the learned state or in the unlearned state." "There is no forgetting; rules do not make the transition in the other direction." (p. 260). Its parameters come from earlier students: "These parameters are estimated empirically for each rule." (p. 261); "The two learning and two performance parameter estimates were held constant across the twenty-one rules and the four values were estimated from an earlier group of students." (p. 264). Mastery: "That mastery criterion in the tutor is a knowledge probability of 0.95." (p. 261). The outcome: "If we adopt a strict operational definition of mastery as 90% correct on the test, Figure 7 indicates that 56% of the students in this knowledge tracing condition reached the mastery performance level." against "Only 24% percent of these students reached this high level of test performance." in the comparison group (p. 274).
- **PFA** (Pavlik et al.): a logistic model of each knowledge component's easiness and the student's prior successes and failures on it, "s tracks the prior successes for the KC for the student, f tracks the prior failures for the KC for the student, and γ and ρ scale the effect of these observation counts." (PDF p. 4), fitted on labelled data: "All of these datasets came with preset domain content KC labels for each performance." (PDF p. 4). On BKT: "Secondary analysis suggested that the KT model assumption that a performance error indicates that (unless a slip occurred) the KC is unlearned was an exaggeration of the data." (PDF p. 7).
- **DKT** (Piech et al.): a recurrent network over exercise tags and correctness, with one output per exercise; "The dataset included 1.4 million exercises completed by 47,495 students across 69 different exercise types." (§5); "One disadvantage of RNNs over simple hidden Markov methods is that they require large amounts of training data, and so are well suited to an online education environment, but not a small classroom environment." (§7).

**What paper A takes.** All three need what MathTrail lacks: items or skills that recur across many learners, and the pooled data of earlier learners to fit them. A task written once for one child has no identity that recurs, and MathTrail keeps no database [C002]. What fits is the learner's half of the logistic Elo family, which updates online from one child's answers (section 2). PFA's finding that a continuous strength reacts to one error more gently than a binary state supports that choice. That a task written at a level and difficulty has the intended difficulty is an assumption no method here can check without pooled answers; paper A says so [C013].

## 7. Mastery criteria

**Keys:** `pelanek2017experimental`, `pelanek2018conceptual`, `kelly2015defining`, `fancsali2013optimal`, `doroudi2020mastery`.

- **Simple criteria do about as well as models, tuned.** "The optimized thresholds are between 0.9 and 0.97 for BKT and between 2 and 8 for NCC. With respect to wMAD, BKT is typically better, but the difference is not large." (Pelánek and Řihák, PDF p. 4). "Our results suggest that there is not a fundamental difference between simple mastery criteria (consecutive correct, exponential moving average) and more complex methods based on the use of learner modeling techniques. The important decisions are what data to use for mastery decision and the choice of thresholds." (PDF p. 7). Mastery should be revisited: "Instead of treating mastery as a permanent state, it would be better to treat it as a temporary state that needs reassessment." (PDF p. 7). Their weighting of errors: "we consider weighted mean absolute deviation (wMAD), where we put w times more weight to under-practice than to over-practice (we use w = 5 unless state otherwise)." (PDF p. 4). In two of their slow-learning logistic scenarios the best N was 17 and 15 (Table 3).
- **A run is weak under noise.** "The NCC criterion has worse performance when observations are noisy (parameter a in the LogisticHMM model is low). Intuitively, this is because the NCC criterion has very limited “memory” – once incorrect answer is observed, the counting of correct answers starts from zero and all information about previous attempts is forbidden." (Pelánek 2018, PDF p. 6). And BKT's 0.95 is meaningful only for binary knowledge: "If we use the common 0.95 threshold under these circumstances, BKT model leads to premature declaration of mastery for all learners." (PDF p. 8).
- **A run's hidden model.** "In particular, we show that the N-Consecutive Correct in a Row heuristic and a simplified version of ALEKS’ mastery learning heuristic are both optimal policies for variants of the Bayesian knowledge tracing model." "Using Bayes’ rule, it can be shown that this implies that the probability of slipping P(S) must be zero." (Doroudi 2020).
- **Three in a row, measured.** "An incremental efficiency analysis reveals that a threshold of 3 consecutive correct responses provides adequate practice, especially for students who reach the threshold without making an error." (Kelly et al., abstract); "Table 1 shows that 80% of students who answer three questions correctly, go on to answer the fourth and fifth correctly as well." (PDF p. 1); for transfer, "However, when predicting performance on a transfer task, a higher threshold (5-CCR) is more effective." (PDF p. 2). Tens of students, one transfer item.
- **The threshold as a classifier.** "Viewed from this perspective, the mastery threshold can be viewed as a parameter that controls the relative frequency of false negatives and false positives." (Fancsali et al., abstract); "Over all three simulation scenarios, we find that the conventional 95% mastery threshold probability leads to pre-mature mastery judgment for under 5% of students per skill for the majority of cases." (PDF p. 7).

**What paper A takes.** MathTrail's rule — at least five answers in the topic, then three qualifying answers since the last wrong one, a qualifying answer being correct, unaided and on a task with P ≤ 0.775; lost after two wrong in a row; held at a level [C015, C082] — fits Pelánek's framing: the gate sets the degree of knowledge, the five answers bound the uncertainty, the run is the evidence, and the loss rule makes mastery a state to reassess. Three is a value in use, at the low end of the tuned ones; Kelly et al. found it predicted the next answers well, and their transfer data favour five. The tension is with MathTrail's own target: its tasks are set where the model expects a child to fail about one time in four or five, the noisy regime in which a run is weakest, and a run assumes a learner who never slips. Paper A states the rule as a design choice, cites this literature for both its support and its tension, and leaves the rate of false mastery to S38's simulation [TBD: S38], with Fancsali et al.'s vocabulary of premature mastery against over-practice.

## 8. The target success rate

**Keys:** `wilson2019eighty`, `papousek2016impact`, and the experiments of `pelanek2017elobased`.

- **The 85 % rule.** "For all of these stochastic gradient-descent based learning algorithms, we find that the optimal error rate for training is around 15.87% or, conversely, that the optimal training accuracy is about 85%." (Wilson et al., abstract). Under its assumption of Gaussian noise: "We further assume that this noise, n, is random and sampled from a zero-mean Gaussian distribution with standard deviation σ (Fig. 1a)." Other noise moves it: "For example, as shown in the Methods, relaxing the assumption that the noise is Gaussian leads to changes in the optimal training accuracy: from 85% for Gaussian, to 82% for Laplacian noise, to 75% for Cauchy noise (Eq. (31) in the “Methods”)." Its stated limits: "As such our work represents the first step towards a more mathematical instantiation of these theories, although it remains to be generalized to a broader class of circumstances, such as multi-choice tasks and different learning algorithms." (Discussion).
- **Experiments in a geography system.** Papoušek et al. (2016): "The experiment shows that easy questions are better for short term engagement, whereas difficult questions are better for long term engagement and learning." (abstract), with almost 3,300,000 answers from about 37,000 learners; the authors contrast this with mathematics: "These results are in contrast with previous research [2,6], which may be due to different learning domain (procedural knowledge in mathematics vs. declarative knowledge in geography)." (PDF p. 5). Pelánek et al.: "This experiment therefore suggests that values around 65% may be a suitable target rate for this kind of application." (PDF p. 26), while users in schools preferred "easier questions with the optimal difficulty being around 75%" (PDF p. 27).
- **Math Garden's .75** is in section 2. A Math Garden experiment comparing 60, 75 and 90 % (Jansen et al. 2013) is known here only through Pelánek et al. and is not cited.

**What paper A takes.** MathTrail's corridor [0.70, 0.85] [C014] holds all three of Wilson et al.'s optima, 75, 82 and 85 %, and its aim of 0.775 lies between the first two; the rule supports holding success at a fixed level somewhere there, not 0.775 as such. It is proven for binary choices and gradient learners; five options with a floor and children solving olympiad problems are outside it, as the draft already says. The experiments are on geography facts and arithmetic, and they point different ways; paper A cites them as context for a band, not as evidence for its value.

## 9. Stochastic approximation

**Key:** `robbins1951stochastic`.

- The procedure finds the point where a response curve takes a given value, from noisy answers. Its steps: "Let {a_n} be a fixed sequence of positive constants such that (6) 0 < Sum_{1}^{∞} a_n^2 = A < ∞." (p. 401), and "(26) Sum_{n=2}^{∞} a_n/(a_1 + ... + a_{n-1}) = ∞." (p. 403), from which Σa_n = ∞ follows. "We shall call any sequence {a_n} which satisfies (6) and (26), whether or not it is of the form (30), a sequence of type 1/n." (p. 404), with "(30) c'/n <= a_n <= c''/n" (p. 404).
- The result holds "no matter what the initial value x_1. As is well known, (10) implies the convergence in probability of x_n to θ." (p. 401). For binary responses the recursion estimates a quantile of the response curve without assuming its form; the authors claim no efficiency: "For any given F(x) there doubtless exist more efficient estimators of θ than any of the type {x_n} defined by (45), but {x_n} has the advantage of being distribution-free." (p. 406).

**What paper A takes.** MathTrail's step K₀/(1 + 0.05n) lies between K₀/(1.05n) and 20K₀/n for n ≥ 1, so it is "of type 1/n". The 1951 paper assumes conditions (6) and (26), not the textbook pair; the draft's Section 5.2 should say "of type 1/n" rather than name the textbook conditions. Under idealisations — one topic, every task served at exactly P = 0.775 on a continuous scale, a child who does not change — the rule is their recursion for the point where the child succeeds 77.5 % of the time, which converges there whatever the shape of the child's response curve, provided the curve never falls as tasks get easier and crosses 0.775 with a positive slope (their conditions (33)–(35)). The ladder's fifteen discrete points, the corridor, the level θ shared across topics and a child who learns all break the idealisation; S38 measures the rule as it is.

## 10. Language models as learner models

**Keys:** `hooshyar2026large`, `neshaei2024modeling`.

- **The argument.** "This assumption is flawed because LLMs do not construct or maintain a learner model that is an accurate, explicit, data-driven representation of a student’s knowledge states over time." (Hooshyar et al., arXiv §1).
- **The study.** Llama 3 8B against deep knowledge tracing on next-answer prediction: "Our findings show that DKT achieves the highest discrimination performance (AUC = 0.83) on next-step correctness prediction and consistently outperforms the LLM across evaluation settings." (arXiv abstract). Zero-shot, the model's updates often went the wrong way: "By contrast, the baseline LLM variant exhibits clear temporal weaknesses, showing higher inconsistency (0.5012), meaning that many of its updates contradict what the student’s response suggests." (arXiv §4.1). Their conclusion: "Rather than replacing learner modelling, LLMs may be more appropriately deployed as pedagogical interfaces or content generators paired with dedicated learner modelling components to ensure responsible, accurate, reliable, and pedagogically sound support." (IEEE Access abstract). Its own limit: "Importantly, our evaluation in this study isolates an LLM-only condition for controlled comparison with DKT." (IEEE Access §V).
- **Another test.** Fine-tuned, a model matched the classic tracer: "While LLM-based approaches do not achieve state-of-the-art performance, fine-tuned LLMs surpass the performance of naive baseline models and perform on par with standard Bayesian Knowledge Tracing approaches across multiple metrics." (Neshaei et al., abstract). Zero-shot it did not: "In particular, the zero-shot approach failed to beat any of the naive baselines on any meaningful metric, while the fine-tuned LLMs achieved higher AUC than all the naive baselines across all three datasets." (§6). The best tracers stayed ahead: "The best performing models we tried from previous works were DKT and Best-LR, achieving AUC scores ranging from 0.04 to 0.13 above the fine-tuned LLMs on extended prompts across datasets." (§5.3).

**What paper A takes.** The published abstract of Hooshyar et al. describes MathTrail's division of labour: the chat's model writes, a dedicated learner model decides. The evidence is narrow — Hooshyar et al. tested one 8B model on one dataset, Neshaei et al. a fine-tuned GPT-3 and GPT-3.5 zero-shot on three, all given identifiers or counts of answers rather than task texts — and mixed: fine-tuned, a model matched BKT, though not the best tracers. It supports keeping the learner model out of a general chat model's hands, which the service can neither fine-tune nor pin, not that MathTrail's hybrid is better. MathTrail's own reasons are its own: the host's model changes and cannot be pinned or fine-tuned by the service, and a rule the service runs is deterministic and can be replayed [C003, C018].

## Answers

**1. Where the model comes from.** P = 0.2 + 0.8σ(θ + δ_topic − β) is Birnbaum's three-parameter logistic model with Rasch's slope and a floor fixed at one in five. Its update is the hierarchical Elo of adaptive practice (Nižnan et al.; Pelánek et al.), with a designed rather than learned item difficulty. Paper A should call it that and cite those works as the direct precedent.

**2. The step.** K₀/(1 + 0.05n) is Papoušek et al.'s uncertainty function, fitted on first answers to geography questions. It is stable for a learner who stays put and lags one who learns; Math Garden now floors a learner's K, Glicko recommends a floor, and Glicko-2 lets uncertainty grow back. MathTrail's has no floor. With a constant step, fixed items and a target above one half, Elo was found biased low; whether MathTrail's shrinking step keeps that bias, and what the lag costs, S38 measures.

**3. The Bayesian trackers** give the step a meaning, uncertainty, but none of the four read in full has, as published, a guessing floor; MV-Glicko's abstract is silent on it. Urnings discard observations a child cannot spare.

**4. General plus topic levels.** MathTrail's θ + δ is a one-parameter bifactor structure with a guessing parameter; the topic levels converge slowly in every simulation read, and their split from θ is set by the steps, not by the answers.

**5. Knowledge tracing** needs recurring items and pooled data; MathTrail has neither, by design.

**6. Mastery.** A short run is a defensible criterion, often used in practice; the threshold and the data matter more than the model; mastery should be reassessable. MathTrail's run of three on tasks set at a 77.5 % success rate is in the noisy regime where a run is weakest, and its rate of false mastery has to be simulated.

**7. The target.** The 85 % rule supports a fixed success level between 75 and 85 % for binary choices and gradient learners; MathTrail's corridor holds that band, and its aim is a choice within it.

**8. Stochastic approximation.** The step is of type 1/n; under idealisations the rule is a Robbins–Monro recursion for the child's 77.5 % point, which converges whatever the curve's shape, provided it never falls as tasks get easier and crosses that point with a positive slope, so long as the child does not change.

**9. Language models** are weaker learner models than the dedicated ones on the evidence available, which is narrow: zero-shot they fall below simple baselines, and fine-tuned they reach BKT but not the best tracers. The division of labour MathTrail uses is the one Hooshyar et al. recommend.
