# Olympiad problems as a means, and how to measure what they teach

The notes of task S10 (`protocol.md`, section 1). The questions:
- what olympiad-style practice, and the tradition of circles and competitions behind it, is known to do;
- how the large assessments define solving a problem one has not met before;
- how research measures transfer, the novelty of a task for a learner, and the skill itself;
- where computational thinking meets the heuristics of problem solving.

The notes end with how paper A can make "olympiad-style" and "new to the child" operational, for its introduction and for S40.

The searches are in `search-log.tsv` (task S10), in English and in Russian. Ten works were read for this area. The notes also draw on two read for S09: Barnett and Ceci on transfer, and *Adding It Up* on nonroutine problems (`problem-solving.md`).

## Preparing primary-school children for an olympiad

**Key:** `rebholz2022getting`. The only study found of olympiad-style practice in primary school that has a comparison group.

**What it did.**
- A course of ten content modules prepared third- and fourth-graders for the German Mathematical Olympiad. It combined games, open problems solved alone, then in pairs, then shared with the group, and written solutions.
- The children were nominated by their teachers, and the comparison group entered the same Olympiad without the course. Nomination, not chance, decided who took the course, so the groups were not randomised.
- The outcomes were Olympiad performance, a curriculum-oriented test and motivation.

**Read:** in full, the published version (CC BY) in Loughborough University's repository. The journal's pages.

- The design: "Its effectiveness was evaluated in a quasi-experimental pre- and posttest design (N = 201 students)." (abstract, p. 1175). "In addition to a group of children who were nominated by their teachers to attend our extracurricular math course, we implemented a control group consisting of students who participated in the MO but were not nominated for the training course and did not formally train for it." (p. 1182).
- The tasks: "The mathematical problems the students worked on were not difficult per se (e.g., calculations did not involve numbers larger than 100); rather, the problems were challenging mostly because they consisted of open problems that allowed for multiple solution strategies and required problem solving, modeling, and reasoning." "All tasks included in the math course were custom made and could be solved in several possible ways (an example task used in the math course is shown in Fig. 2). Original tasks from previous MOs were not implemented in the math course." (p. 1181).
- The state of research: "the effects of training for academic competitions on students' performance in the respective competition and on their domain-specific competences and motivation have hardly been investigated so far." (p. 1182).
- The groups at the start: "On average, the children in the training group showed higher (fluid and crystallized) cognitive ability, higher mathematical competence measured with a curriculum-oriented test, higher levels of math selfconcept, and higher levels of intrinsic value, attainment value, and task-specific interest in mathematics" (p. 1188). Table 1 puts the difference in mathematical competence at about one standard deviation in both grades (p. 1189).
- On the Olympiad: "Thus, students participating in the math course performed between about half of a standard deviation and one standard deviation better in the MO than students who did not attend the preparatory math course, controlling for baseline differences between the two groups." (p. 1188).
- On the curriculum test: "fourth-grade students attending the math course scored almost half of a standard deviation higher on this measure than students who did not attend the course after controlling for baseline differences" (p. 1189). The same paragraph: "the treatment effect was statistically significant only for the fourth graders" (p. 1189).
- The authors' reading: "This indicates that by focusing on solving (challenging) open mathematical problems, students acquired competences that were helpful for solving new mathematical problems in the MO." (p. 1191).
- The limits they state: "Students in the training group showed mathematical competences that were above average." "We cannot say with certainty that we were able to control for all relevant pre-treatment differences." (p. 1193). On motivation: "Whereas third graders (who were trained in groups with higher achieving fourth graders) tended to experience a decrease in their math self-concept, the opposite seemed to occur for fourth-grade students (who were trained with lower achieving third graders) as they experienced an increase in their self-concept." (p. 1192).

**What paper A takes.**
- This is the evidence closest to MathTrail's setting: grades 3–4, olympiad-style problems that need no knowledge beyond the grade, and an outcome on a test unlike the course.
- It is also narrow. The children were selected, high-achieving and taught by a teacher with peers. The groups differed by a standard deviation before the course, and the design was quasi-experimental. The authors' "effects" are differences after adjustment, not randomised effects.
- MathTrail's child works alone, in a chat, with no teacher, and is not selected. The study does not show that such practice helps such a child. What it shows: selected children who took one taught course did better than the comparison group on the Olympiad, and fourth-graders on a school test.

## The tradition of competitions and circles, and what is known of its effects

**Keys:** `delosada2022perspectives`, `kenderov2022mathematics`, `leikin2021practice`.

**Falk de Losada and Taylor.** The introduction to a *ZDM* issue on competitions: history, kinds of competitions, countries, careers of olympians. (Crossref records her name as given "María Falk", family "de Losada"; the notes use the name as her papers print it.)

*Read:* in full, in PubMed Central; the sections are named, since that text carries no pages.
- "Mathematical problem-solving competitions for school (primary, secondary or university) have enjoyed over 125 years of vigorous existence." ("Competitions as an example of design science").
- On problems, reporting Freudenthal (1969): "He underlines the fact that a problem can be very simple but, in being unusual for its time, it tests inventiveness rather than rote, and notes that it is not easy to find problems that are both new and not forbidding." ("Some brief reflections on historical roots").
- Two kinds of competition: "These competitions, open to all students and designed to be familiar from classroom experience, are called inclusive." Competitions entered by invitation are called exclusive ("Some definitions").
- Russia: "The next generation of competitions would be the Olympiads of Moscow and Leningrad, in the USSR during the 1930s." ("Some definitions"). And: "Circles were informal groups of students mentored by local teachers committed to continuing the history of high quality of mathematics in a way which schools, which catered for the whole population, could not do." ("Russia").
- Who competes at the top: "the International Mathematical Olympiad (IMO), in which teams of the top six students from each participating country gather in one location" ("Some definitions").
- Careers of German olympians: "Of those known, 148 obtained mathematics-based doctorates and a further 50 studied mathematics and pursued mathematics-based careers." ("Competitions and career choice"). The teams are the top six of each country, so the figure describes a selected group; it does not measure an effect of competing.

**Kenderov.** An overview, in the same *ZDM* issue, of how competitions grew into a worldwide system, with the arguments for and against them.

*Read:* in full, in PubMed Central; sections named.
- "Debate 'for' and 'against' competitions has accompanied the competition ecosystem from its very beginning." Among the points that, in his words, "seem to have general agreement": "The preparation of students for the competition has a significant educational impact." ("Mathematics competitions. Pros and cons.").
- Among the arguments against: "Competitions create a stressful atmosphere, because the problems have to be solved in a short time and in the presence of other competitors." "Competitions at school level convey an inadequate impression that mathematics is a collection of problems." (same section).

**Leikin.** A review of research and practice in educating the mathematically gifted, from Soviet practice of 1960–1980 to today.

*Read:* in full, in PubMed Central; sections named.
- The evidence: "research reports and papers in high-level research journals in mathematics education and gifted education that present systematic studies on the development of mathematical talent are rare. More research is needed to explain the mechanisms of mathematical talent development and the impact of educational programs for mathematically gifted students." ("Mathematical enrichment—mathematical circles, competitions and Olympiads").
- The tasks: "Solving unconventional tasks usually is directed at the activation and development of students' mathematical creativity, since it requires students to implement their knowledge and skills in new situations or to construct new ideas for solving the problems." ("Mathematical content for the promotion of mathematical giftedness").
- The Soviet emphasis, reporting Sharygin: "Sharygin (1989) recommended 'changing priorities': these changes included prioritizing ideas when learning a new topic and solving non-routine (heuristic) problems, vs. prioritizing complete answers when working with known ideas and solving standard problems." (same section).

**What paper A takes.**
- Olympiad-style problems come from a long and wide tradition. Paper A can cite it as the source of its genre, not as evidence of an effect.
- That tradition mostly served selected, gifted students, and even for them systematic studies of its effects are rare (Leikin). Kenderov's "general agreement" on the educational impact of preparation is the view of the field's practitioners, not a measured result.
- Inclusive competitions are, by definition, "open to all students": the genre is offered to children who were not selected. What it does for them is not studied in the works read.
- Freudenthal's remark, as Falk de Losada and Taylor report it, names MathTrail's supply problem: problems both new and not forbidding are hard to find.
- Kenderov's two objections bear on MathTrail. Without a clock or an audience it avoids the first. It may still convey the second: a stream of tasks is a collection of problems.

## What an olympiad score measures

**Key:** `agarwal2020invisible`.

**What it did.** Linked the scores of International Mathematical Olympiad participants to their later degrees, publications, citations and honours, and compared medallists with mathematicians who never took part.

**Read:** in full, the authors' accepted manuscript in the University of Bath's repository. The page numbers are the manuscript's PDF pages.
- "First, individuals who excelled in teenage years are especially capable of advancing the knowledge frontier." (abstract, p. 2).
- "Strong IMO performers are significantly more likely to produce frontier research in mathematics than either lesser IMO performers or mathematicians who did not participate in the IMO." (p. 4).
- "Furthermore, the number of points score in more difficult problems is more predictive of future achievements than the number of points scored in the easier problems (appendix Table A3)." (p. 12).
- The authors' own caution: "We also have to allow for the possibility that IMO scores may be correlated with performance in mathematics research even if IMO scores are uninformative about talent. That scenario could occur if IMO performance had a causal effect on performance." (p. 11).

**What paper A takes.** Scores on hard olympiad problems predict later mathematical achievement, at the very top of the ability distribution and among the "high-school students" the competition is for (p. 3). This supports olympiad problems as a measure of something that matters. It does not show that practising them builds it, and it says nothing about primary-school children.

## Problem solving in PISA and TIMSS

**Keys:** `oecd2013pisa`, `philpot2021timss` (in `web.bib`: the framework has no DOI).

**PISA 2012.** The framework of the computer-based assessment of problem solving for 15-year-olds.

*Read:* chapter 4's definition, pp. 122–123, from the page images of a scan of the book at the Internet Archive. The scan's text layer was only used to find the pages; the quotes below were checked against the images.
- "Problem-solving competency is an individual's capacity to engage in cognitive processing to understand and resolve problem situations where a method of solution is not immediately obvious. It includes the willingness to engage with such situations in order to achieve one's potential as a constructive and reflective citizen." (p. 122).
- "The status of a problem – whether it is routine or not – depends on the solver's familiarity with the problem. A "problem" for one person may have an obvious solution to another person who is experienced and practised in solving such problems. Accordingly, care was taken to set problems that should be non-routine for the great majority of 15-year-olds." (p. 123).
- "It is not necessarily the case that the context or goals will themselves be unfamiliar to the solver; what is important is that the particular problems are novel or the ways of achieving the goals are not immediately obvious." (p. 123).
- "The PISA 2012 problem-solving assessment will not include problems that require expert knowledge for their solution." (p. 123).

**TIMSS 2023.** The mathematics framework for grades 4 and 8.

*Read:* in full, the chapter on the publisher's site. The framework's pages.
- The reasoning domain: "Reasoning mathematically involves logical, systematic thinking. It includes intuitive and inductive reasoning based on patterns and regularities that can be used to arrive at solutions to problems." (p. 16).
- Its share of grade 4: Knowing 40 %, Applying 40 %, Reasoning 20 % of the score points (Exhibit 1.4, p. 15).

**What paper A takes.**
- PISA defines what MathTrail calls olympiad-style from the solver's side. The method is "not immediately obvious", and whether a problem is routine "depends on the solver's familiarity with the problem". That is the ground for making novelty relative to the child.
- PISA also rules out "expert knowledge", as MathTrail's working definition rules out knowledge beyond the grade [Q15].
- TIMSS gives reasoning a fifth of the grade-4 assessment. The skill is assessed at this age, and it is a minority of what is assessed.
- MathTrail's tasks are neither PISA's interactive problems nor TIMSS items. The notes borrow definitions, not instruments.

## Novelty and transfer: what to measure

**Keys:** `holyoak1987surface`; `barnett2002where` (S09).

**Holyoak and Koh.** Two experiments on when solvers retrieve and use an earlier problem as an analogue.

*Read:* the abstract and the opening of the first page (p. 332), from the scan the authors' laboratory posts, read as an image. The experiments themselves were not read.
- "The results of Experiment 2 indicated that both structural features, which play a causal role in determining possible problem solutions, and salient surface features, which do not have a causal role, influence spontaneous selection of an analogue. Structural features, however, have a greater impact than do surface features on a problem solver's ability to use an analogue once its relevance has been pointed out." (abstract, p. 332).
- "A person confronted by a novel problem can sometimes solve it by drawing an analogy to a similar problem that has a known solution." (p. 332).

**Barnett and Ceci** (read for S09, abstract and introduction) name what a study of transfer has to state: "the nature of the skill to be transferred, the performance change measured for this skill, and the memory demands of the transfer task used to measure it" and "the distance between the training and transfer contexts along multiple dimensions" (p. 614; `problem-solving.md`, "Transfer").

**What paper A takes.**
- A task can be new in two ways. Its surface can be new: the story, the objects, the numbers. Its structure can be new: the idea that solves it.
- MathTrail's near-duplicate check sees only the surface, and only close copies. It rejects a question whose trigram Jaccard index is 0.7 or more against a reference question of the child's level, or against one of up to 200 of the child's past tasks, where the index is estimated from their MinHash sketches [C027–C029]. A task with a new story on an idea the child has met passes the check. Holyoak and Koh suggest it may still be less new to the child, and that the child may or may not notice the shared idea.
- Rebholz et al. measured an outcome at a distance from the course, the curriculum-oriented test, besides the Olympiad itself. In Barnett and Ceci's terms, the curriculum test sits at a greater distance from the training than the Olympiad does.
- Paper A measures no transfer. It should describe the near-duplicate check as a guard against repeated text, not as a measure of novelty for the child.

## Computational thinking and the heuristics of problem solving

**Keys:** `wing2006computational`, `voogt2015computational`.

**Wing.** The essay that made the term current.

*Read:* in full, from a copy on a Carnegie Mellon course page. The magazine's pages.
- "Computational thinking is a fundamental skill for everyone, not just for computer scientists." (p. 33).
- "Computational thinking is reformulating a seemingly difficult problem into one we know how to solve, perhaps by reduction, embedding, transformation, or simulation." (p. 33).
- "Computational thinking is using abstraction and decomposition when attacking a large complex task or designing a large complex system." (p. 33).

**Voogt et al.** A review of how computational thinking is defined and taught in compulsory education.

*Read:* in full, the published version (CC BY) in the University of Amsterdam's repository. The journal's pages.
- On earlier hopes of transfer from programming: "However, results from the studies surrounding LOGO were decidedly inconclusive." (p. 717). "This aligns with the critiques by Salomon and Perkins (1989) in that the instructional methods and their focus on the general skills is the more effective strategy for ensuring transfer, not a focus on computer programming itself." (p. 718).
- Barr and Stephenson's nine concepts, as the review reports them: "They suggested nine core concepts for CT in K-12 education: data collection, data analysis, data representation, problem decomposition, abstraction, algorithm and procedures, automation, parallelization and simulation." (p. 721).

**What paper A takes.**
- Computational thinking names moves whose names also appear among the articles of Pólya's dictionary (`problem-solving.md`, contents only):
  - decomposition, as his "Decomposing and recombining";
  - reformulating a problem into a known one, as his "Do you know a related problem?";
  - abstraction, next to his "Generalization".
- MathTrail practises these moves on paper-and-pencil problems. Computational thinking practises them on problems meant for a computer.
- The history the review recounts is a caution for MathTrail's own claim. Transfer from programming to general thinking was expected, and the studies were "decidedly inconclusive". So paper A should not promise transfer from olympiad practice to general thinking either (S09, Sala and Gobet).

## The area's answers

**1. What olympiad-style practice is known to do.**
- The genre is old and widespread, and its tradition in the Soviet Union and Russia is documented (Falk de Losada and Taylor; Leikin).
- Its effects are little studied. Systematic studies are rare even for the gifted (Leikin), and those of training for competitions have "hardly been investigated" (Rebholz et al.). Practitioners agree that preparation has an educational impact (Kenderov), which is an opinion, not a measurement.
- One quasi-experiment with selected third- and fourth-graders taught in a group found them ahead of a comparison group on the Olympiad, and in grade 4 on a school test (Rebholz et al.).
- Olympiad scores at the top predict later mathematical achievement (Agarwal and Gaule). Careers of olympians describe a selected group. Neither measures an effect of practice.

The sentence paper A's introduction now carries: olympiad-style problems come from a long tradition of circles and competitions, whose effects have seldom been studied systematically. The one study with a comparison group found in primary school, with selected children taught in a group course and groups that differed before it, found them ahead in the competition and, in grade 4, on a curriculum test.

**2. How PISA and TIMSS define the skill.** PISA 2012 defines problem solving by a method that is "not immediately obvious", relative to the solver's familiarity, and without expert knowledge. TIMSS gives reasoning a fifth of the grade-4 test. Neither provides an instrument MathTrail uses.

**3. How transfer, novelty and the skill are measured.**
- Transfer is measured on a task at a stated distance from those practised (Barnett and Ceci), as Rebholz et al. used a curriculum test besides the competition.
- Novelty for a learner is relative to what the learner has met (PISA). It has a surface and a structure (Holyoak and Koh).
- The skill at the top is measured by olympiad scores, whose harder problems predict more (Agarwal and Gaule).
- No instrument was found for how novel a task is for a given learner. It is a gap, logged as a query that found nothing.

**4. Where computational thinking meets the heuristics.**
- In decomposition, reformulation into a known problem, and abstraction (Wing; Voogt et al., reporting Barr and Stephenson).
- The task also names patterns. None of the computational-thinking works read lists pattern recognition among its components. Patterns enter the notes through mathematics instead: TIMSS's "reasoning based on patterns and regularities", and Pólya's "Specialization" and "Generalization" in S09.
- Its own record on transfer is a caution (Voogt et al.).

**5. Operational definitions for paper A and S40.**
- *Olympiad-style.* The working definition is a default the executor set [Q15], turned into a rubric [Q35]; the author's own criteria are still asked [Q46]. The works read give sources for parts of it:
  - "the method is not known in advance" is PISA's "a method of solution is not immediately obvious", judged for the child's grade;
  - "the solution rests on an idea" is Leikin's unconventional task that asks the student "to construct new ideas";
  - "no knowledge beyond the grade" is PISA's exclusion of "expert knowledge", and Rebholz et al.'s tasks that were "not difficult per se". "Self-contained", the other half of that criterion, has no source here;
  - "short once the idea is found" has no source among the works read.
- *New to the child*, at two levels:
  - *surface*: the text is not a close copy of a reference task or of one of the child's recent tasks, with "close" and "recent" as the service defines them [C027–C029]. This is what the service checks, and paper A should call it that.
  - *structure*: the idea has not been used in this child's recent tasks. The service does not check it; the topic and the traps of a task approximate it [C019, C020], and a reviewer can judge it. For S40, if E-A2 is ever run: a blind reviewer marks whether a task's idea repeats one of the child's earlier tasks, and the rate is reported next to the textual check.
- *Transfer*: paper A measures none. A later study would measure it on tasks at a stated distance from those practised, as Rebholz et al. used a curriculum test.
