## Pólya: questions a solver asks, and a teacher asks the student

**Key:** `polya2014solve`. The Princeton Science Library edition of 2014; the book first appeared in 1945.

**What it is.** A method of solving problems set out as a list of questions and suggestions. The list is organised in four phases: understanding the problem, devising a plan, carrying it out, and looking back. A "dictionary" of heuristics follows.

**Read:** only the sample the publisher posts, the contents and the introduction (pp. xi–xxvii). The heuristics themselves were not read. So the notes rest nothing on how Pólya describes any single move, only on the book's structure and on the purpose of the list.

- The contents list, among the "Main divisions, main questions", the sections "Four phases", "Understanding the problem", "Devising a plan", "Carrying out the plan" and "Looking back". Among the dictionary's articles are "Specialization", "Generalization", "Decomposing and recombining", "Working backwards", "Do you know a related problem?", "Draw a figure", "Did you use all the data?", "What is the unknown?" and "Can you check the result?" (Contents).
- The purpose of the list: "If, using them properly, you address these questions and suggestions to yourself, they may help you to solve your problem. If, using them properly, you address the same questions and suggestions to one of your students, you may help him to solve his problem." (Introduction, p. xxv).

**What paper A takes.**
- The introduction cites the book for its four phases and for two moves that the contents name as articles, "Specialization" and "Working backwards". Nothing else in the draft rests on it.
- The same questions serve the solver and the teacher. That is the idea behind a hint phrased as a question rather than as a step of the solution.
- MathTrail's hint is written in advance by the model, and it may be either a leading question or the first step [C061]. Only the question has this grounding. Pólya's teacher chooses the question for the student in front of him.

## Schoenfeld: resources, heuristics, control, beliefs

**Key:** `schoenfeld2016learning`. The 1992 chapter in the *Handbook for Research on Mathematics Teaching and Learning*, reprinted in the *Journal of Education* in 2016; the reprint is the version with a DOI.

**What it did.** A review of research on mathematical thinking and problem solving up to 1992. It uses a framework of five aspects, and within them reports Schoenfeld's own problem-solving courses and a metacognition study with seventh-graders.

**Read:** in full, from a copy of the manuscript posted with a course at the University of Georgia (`jwilson.coe.uga.edu/emat7050/schoenfeld_maththinking.pdf`). The page numbers below are the manuscript's, not the reprint's.

- *The framework.* "there appears to be general agreement on the importance of these five aspects of cognition: • The knowledge base • Problem solving strategies • Monitoring and control • Beliefs and affects • Practices." (p. 42).
- *Routine exercises are not problems.* "They are, rather, routine exercises organized to provide practice on a particular mathematical technique that, typically, has just been demonstrated to the student." (p. 11).
- *Why Pólya's heuristics did not teach themselves.* "In short, the critique of the strategies listed in How to Solve It and its successors is that the characterizations of them were descriptive rather than prescriptive. That is, the characterizations allowed one to recognize the strategies when they were being used. However, Pólya's characterizations did not provide the amount of detail that would enable people who were not already familiar with the strategies to be able to implement them." (p. 53). And: "The indications are (see, e.g., Schoenfeld, 1985a) that students can learn to use these more carefully delineated strategies." (p. 55).
- *Control.* "Monitoring and assessing progress "on line," and acting in response to the assessments of on-line progress, are the core components of self-regulation." (p. 58). "Their results can be summed up as follows: it's not just what you know; it's how, when, and whether you use it." (p. 60).
- *What goes wrong without it.* In more than a hundred videotaped attempts at unfamiliar problems, "roughly sixty percent of the solution attempts are of the "read, make a decision quickly, and pursue that direction come hell or high water" variety. And that first, quick, wrong decision, if not reconsidered and reversed, guarantees failure." (p. 61).
- *Coaching by questions.* The instructor "reserves the right to ask the following three questions at any time: What (exactly) are you doing? (Can you describe it precisely?) Why are you doing it? (How does it fit into the solution?) How does it help you? (What will you do with the outcome when you obtain it?)" (p. 63). "(Note, however, that the better part of a semester is necessary to obtain such changes.)" (p. 63). After the course, "In contrast to the 60% of the "jump into a solution attempt and pursue it no matter what" attempts prior to the course, fewer than 20% of the post-instruction solution attempts were of that type." (p. 64). Schoenfeld adds that "to a significant degree solving non-standard problems is a matter of luck and prior knowledge." (p. 64).
- *Seventh-graders (Lester, Garofalo and Kroll, as Schoenfeld reports them).* Among the teaching actions, each with its purpose (the table's two columns joined here by a dash): "5. Provide hints as needed — Help students past blockages" and "7. Require students who obtain a solution to "answer the question" — Require students to look over their work and make sure it makes sense" (Table 2, p. 65). Among the conclusions: "In order for students' problem solving performance to improve, they must attempt to solve a variety of types of problems on a regular basis and over a prolonged period of time." and "Metacognition instruction is most effective when it takes place in a domain specific context." (p. 66).
- *Beliefs.* One of the typical beliefs: "Students who have understood the mathematics they have studied will be able to solve any assigned problem in five minutes or less." (Table 3, p. 69). Its consequence: "Students with the belief will give up working on a problem after a few minutes of unsuccessful attempts, even though they might have solved it had they persevered." (p. 70).

**What paper A takes.** Schoenfeld's distinction between exercises and problems is the one paper A draws between procedures and new problems. What he reports about teaching is a caution for paper A:
- Knowing the heuristics by name was not enough. Students used them once the heuristics were broken into finer strategies and coached over a semester.
- The seventh-grade study asked for many kinds of problems over a long time, and for metacognition taught within the domain.

MathTrail supplies the variety and the regularity. It does not teach heuristics or control, and a hint written in advance is not coaching. The service does not count slowness against a child: pace does not enter the rating [C012]. That fits the belief Schoenfeld warns about, that a problem should take five minutes.

## Adding It Up: strategic competence and adaptive reasoning

**Key:** `anonymous2001adding`. National Research Council, 2001, edited by Kilpatrick, Swafford and Findell. The registry records neither authors nor editors, hence the key.

**What it is.** A consensus report on mathematics learning from pre-kindergarten to grade 8. It defines mathematical proficiency as five intertwined strands: conceptual understanding, procedural fluency, strategic competence, adaptive reasoning and productive disposition.

**Read:** chapter 4, "The Strands of Mathematical Proficiency" (pp. 115–155), in the reader on the publisher's site. The page numbers are the book's, as the site marks them.

- "The five strands are interwoven and interdependent in the development of proficiency in mathematics." (p. 116). Adaptive reasoning is the "capacity for logical thought, reflection, explanation, and justification" (p. 116).
- "Strategic competence refers to the ability to formulate mathematical problems, represent them, and solve them." (p. 124).
- "In contrast, nonroutine problems are problems for which the learner does not immediately know a usable solution method. Nonroutine problems require productive thinking because the learner needs to invent a way to understand and solve the problem." (p. 126).
- "Flexibility of approach is the major cognitive requirement for solving nonroutine problems." (p. 127).
- "The development of strategies for solving nonroutine problems depends on understanding the quantities involved in the problems and their relationships as well as on fluency in solving routine problems." (p. 127).
- On the national assessment of 13-year-olds in the United States: "Given traditional curricula and methods of instruction, students develop proficiency among the five strands in a very uneven way. They are most proficient in aspects of procedural fluency and less proficient in conceptual understanding, strategic competence, adaptive reasoning, and productive disposition." (p. 136).

**What paper A takes.**
- The report names the skill MathTrail practises: strategic competence and adaptive reasoning on nonroutine problems.
- It measures the gap paper A starts from, for American 13-year-olds under traditional instruction.
- It defines a nonroutine problem relative to the learner. A problem whose method the child already knows is no longer nonroutine for that child, which is the argument for a task new to each child.
- It also corrects paper A's opening. Procedures are not the opposite of problem solving but one of its strands, and fluency with routine problems is a condition for strategies on nonroutine ones. The introduction should say that school mathematics develops the strands unevenly, not that procedures are superficial.

## How People Learn: conditionalised knowledge, adaptive expertise, transfer

**Key:** `anonymous2000people`. National Research Council, expanded edition, 2000, edited by Bransford, Brown and Cocking. The registry records no authors here either.

**What it is.** A consensus report on the science of learning. Chapter 2 compares experts with novices; chapter 3 is on learning and transfer.

**Read:** chapters 2 (pp. 31–50) and 3 (pp. 51–78), in the reader on the publisher's site. The page numbers are the book's.

- *Inert knowledge.* "In the language of cognitive scientists, experts' knowledge is "conditionalized"—it includes a specification of the contexts in which it is useful (Simon, 1980; Glaser, 1992). Knowledge that is not conditionalized is often "inert" because it is not activated, even though it is relevant (Whitehead, 1929)." (p. 43).
- *How practice can keep knowledge inert.* "Sometimes, however, students can solve sets of practice problems but fail to conditionalize their knowledge because they know which chapter the problems came from and so automatically use this information to decide which concepts and formulas are relevant. Practice problems that are organized into very structured worksheets can also cause this problem." (p. 43).
- *Adaptive expertise (Hatano and Inagaki, as the report describes it).* "Adaptive experts are able to approach new situations flexibly and to learn throughout their lifetimes. They not only use what they have learned, they are metacognitive and continually question their current levels of expertise and attempt to move beyond them." (p. 48). And a limit: "Experts are not simply "general problem solvers" who have learned a set of strategies that operate across all domains." (p. 48).
- *Transfer.* "When a subject is taught in multiple contexts, however, and includes examples that demonstrate wide application of what is being taught, people are more likely to abstract the relevant features of concepts and to develop a flexible representation of knowledge (Gick and Holyoak, 1983)." (p. 62). "Simply learning to perform procedures, and learning in only a single context, does not promote flexible transfer. The transfer literature suggests that the most effective transfer may come from a balance of specific examples and general principles, not from either one alone." (p. 77).

**What paper A takes.**
- "Inert knowledge" and "adaptive expertise" enter the introduction from this report. Hatano and Inagaki's chapter itself was not read (Remarks).
- One idea met in many settings is the report's argument for new tasks that share an idea [C013, C028].
- Its warning about practice sorted by chapter bears on the rule. MathTrail changes topic after every correct answer [C019], so the child does not practise one topic in a block. After a wrong answer, though, the next task is on the same topic [C019].
- The limit on "general problem solvers" joins Sala and Gobet below: the skill is mathematical, and paper A should not promise a general one.

## IMPROVE: metacognitive questions that the students ask themselves

**Key:** `kramarski2003enhancing`. Mevarech and Kramarski's own description of IMPROVE (1997) is paywalled; this is their later experiment, which states the method and its questions.

**What it did.** 384 eighth-graders learned graphs of functions under four conditions: cooperative or individual learning, each with or without metacognitive training. The training taught self-addressed comprehension, strategic and connection questions.

**Read:** in full, from a copy of the article posted by a special-interest group of the American Educational Research Association (`ssrlsig.org`).

- The method: "The program is called IMPROVE, the acronym of all the teaching stages: Introducing the new concepts, Metacognitive questioning, Practicing, Reviewing and reducing difficulties, Obtaining mastery, Verification, and Enrichment." (p. 283).
- The questions: "The comprehension questions were designed to prompt students to reflect on a problem before solving it. In addressing a comprehension question, students had to read the problem, describe the relevant concepts in their own words, and try to understand what the concepts meant." "The connection questions were designed to prompt students to focus on similarities and differences between the immediate problem or task and problems or tasks that they had already completed successfully." (p. 286).
- The result: "the metacognitive groups (COOP+META and IND +META) outperformed their counterparts (COOP and IND) on graph construction (transfer tasks) and metacognitive knowledge." (abstract, p. 281). The transfer task was constructing graphs after a unit on reading them, scored 0–7 (Table 5, p. 299). That is transfer within the unit.

**What paper A takes.**
- The questions IMPROVE teaches are the kind of hint MathTrail asks for [C061]: what the problem asks, which strategy fits, what it resembles.
- The difference is who asks. IMPROVE trains students to ask themselves, for every problem, over a unit. MathTrail offers one question for the child to read if they ask for it [C074].
- The study is with eighth-graders; the notes found no such study for grades 1–6.

## Productive failure: problem first, instruction after

**Key:** `sinha2021problem`.

**What it did.** A meta-analysis of 53 studies with 166 comparisons of problem solving followed by instruction (PS-I) against instruction followed by problem solving (I-PS). Productive failure is PS-I built on Kapur's design principles.

**Read:** in full, the published version (CC BY 4.0) in the ETH Zürich Research Collection. The journal's pages.

- "Overall, our results showed a significant effect (Hedge's g) of 0.36 [95% CI 0.20, 0.51] in favor of PS-I (compared to I-PS) for conceptual knowledge and transfer, and a nonsignificant effect (Hedge's g) of −0.03, 95% CI [−0.20, 0.15] for procedural knowledge." (p. 783).
- "Contrasting trends were, however, observed for younger age learners (second to fifth graders) and for the learning of domain-general skills, for which effect sizes favored I-PS." (abstract, p. 761).
- "Younger age students (2nd to 5th graders), however, seemed to benefit from I-PS interventions, although the average effect size favoring I-PS was low (Hedge's g of −0.09 [95% CI −0.92, −0.16]." (p. 786). The same figures stand in Table 4 (p. 780). The point estimate lies outside its own interval (Remarks).
- "We found that effects favoring PS-I over I-PS were higher if students belonged to a higher grade level." (p. 786). For grades 6–10, Table 4 gives "6th to 10th graders (0.50 [−0.04, 0.58])" (p. 780): an interval that reaches below zero.
- "For these younger students then, scaffolding the initial problem-solving phase of PS-I to compensate for their insufficient prior knowledge about cognitive and metacognitive learning strategies might be critical." (p. 786).

**What paper A takes.** MathTrail is closer to problem-first than to instruction-first:
- A task comes with no lesson before it.
- The child reads an explanation of their mistake after answering [C072].

The benefit of problem-first grows with grade, and for grades 6–10 its interval still reaches below zero. For grades 2–5, four of the six grades MathTrail serves, the meta-analysis leans the other way, and its authors ask for scaffolding of the problem-solving phase. So productive failure is not a ground for MathTrail's design in primary school. At most it says that for children of this age, a problem given before any instruction needs support. The hint is the only support the service asks for.

## Desirable difficulties

**Key:** `bjork2020desirable`. The Bjorks' own chapter of 2011 has no DOI; this paper, an introduction to a journal's special issue, restates the idea.

**Read:** in full, the authors' manuscript posted by their laboratory. The page numbers are the manuscript's PDF pages.

- "Desirable difficulties, versus the array of undesirable difficulties, are desirable because they trigger encoding and retrieval processes that support learning, comprehension, and remembering. If, however, the learner does not have the background knowledge or skills to respond to them successfully, they become undesirable difficulties." (p. 3).
- "The level of difficulty that is optimal, therefore, will vary with the degree of a learner's prior learning." (p. 3).
- On spacing and interleaving in mathematics, after Rohrer's classroom studies: "He found strong support for the benefits of spacing, a result that is especially important given that most workbooks, as well as classroom exercises, involved blocked, not interleaved, practice." (p. 5).
- On children: "it remains the case that little research has been focused on the potential of spaced practice to improve children's learning, particularly their learning in a classroom setting." (p. 5).

**What paper A takes.** Difficulty helps only when the learner can meet it, and the level that helps is the learner's own. This is the argument for a difficulty chosen per child rather than per grade [C014]. It does not give a number. The support for mixing topics is real but mostly from older students, and the Bjorks say themselves that it is thin for children.

## Scaffolding and the zone of proximal development

**Key:** `vandepol2010scaffolding`. A review of the decade 1998–2009. Wood, Bruner and Ross (1976), who introduced the term, were not read: the publisher refused automated readers.

**Read:** in full, the published version in the University of Amsterdam's repository. The journal's pages.

- *The zone of proximal development, as the review introduces it.* "Cazden (1979) related Vygotsky's Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD) early on to scaffolding and suggested that the metaphor be expanded from the domain of parent–child interactions to teacher–student interactions. The ZPD is characterized by Vygotsky (1978) as: "the distance between the actual developmental level as determined by independent problem solving and the level of potential development as determined through problem solving under adult guidance or in collaboration with more capable peers" (p. 86)." (p. 272).
- *Contingency.* "The first common characteristic in the various definitions of scaffolding is contingency often referred to as responsiveness, tailored, adjusted, differentiated, titrated, or calibrated support. The teacher's support must be adapted to the current level of the student's performance and should either be at the same or a slightly higher level." (pp. 274–275; the sentence runs across the page break and a figure).
- *Diagnosis first.* "To provide contingent support, that is, one must first determine the student's current level of competence." (p. 275).
- *Fading and transfer of responsibility.* "The second common characteristic is fading or the gradual withdrawal of the scaffolding." "Via contingent fading, that is, responsibility for the performance of a task is gradually transferred to the learner." (p. 275).
- *Not every question is scaffolding.* "Many of the reviewed articles propose specific scaffolding means such as modeling or questioning. However, the use of such strategies does not automatically imply the occurrence of scaffolding, since an interaction in which one of the aforementioned strategies occurs can only be considered scaffolding when the interaction is also characterized by the three key characteristics of scaffolding, namely: contingency, fading, and transfer of responsibility." (p. 275).
- *The evidence.* "The results of studies on students' metacognitive and cognitive activities indicate that scaffolding is effective." and "With this somewhat limited body of effectiveness research on mainly one-to-one tutoring situations with mostly simple and straightforward tasks, future research might start to focus on more naturalistic classroom situations with all sorts of tasks." (p. 283).

**What paper A takes.**
- The review ties scaffolding to the zone of proximal development, and asks for support "at the same or a slightly higher level" than the student's performance. That is the closest this literature comes to "a little harder". The corridor applies the idea to the task rather than to the support.
- By the review's own criterion, a hint fixed in advance is questioning but not scaffolding. It is not contingent: nothing diagnoses the child before it is shown. It does not fade. Paper A should call it a hint, and say that the chat's model, which talks with the child, is the part that could make support contingent. The service does not control that.

## Transfer: how far, and how often

**Keys:** `barnett2002where`, `sala2017far`.

**Barnett and Ceci.** A taxonomy for classifying transfer studies.

*Read:* the abstract and the introduction (pp. 612–614), from a copy of the published article; the rest was skimmed.
- "Despite a century's worth of research, arguments surrounding the question of whether far transfer occurs have made little progress toward resolution. The authors argue the reason for this confusion is a failure to specify various dimensions along which transfer can occur, resulting in comparisons of "apples and oranges."" (abstract, p. 612).
- The dimensions: "(a) the nature of the skill to be transferred, the performance change measured for this skill, and the memory demands of the transfer task used to measure it and (b) the distance between the training and transfer contexts along multiple dimensions (knowledge domain, physical context, temporal context, functional context, social context, and modality)." (p. 614).
- "The past 100 years of research shows that evidence for transfer under some conditions is substantial, but critical conditions for many key questions are untested." (abstract, p. 612).

**Sala and Gobet.** Meta-analyses of chess, music and working-memory training, with their effects on children's cognitive and academic skills.

*Read:* in full, the published version (CC BY 4.0) in LSE Research Online. The journal's pages.
- "The results show small to moderate effects. However, the effect sizes are inversely related to the quality of the experimental design (e.g., presence of active control groups)." (abstract, p. 515).
- "If skills rarely generalize across different domains, then the most effective way to acquire a skill is to train that particular skill." (p. 518).
- "The meta-analytic reviews presented in this article strongly suggest that the optimism about the far-transfer effects of cognitive training is not justified, at least with typically developing children and young adolescents." (p. 518).

**What paper A takes.** The claim has to be sized.
- MathTrail practises solving mathematics problems new to the child, in mathematics. In Barnett and Ceci's terms that is transfer within one knowledge domain, one modality and one setting, the chat. It is neither the far transfer Sala and Gobet find missing nor an improvement in thinking outside mathematics.
- Paper A should not say that olympiad practice makes children better thinkers in general.
- Paper A measures no transfer at all. How transfer could be measured is answered in `olympiad-and-measurement.md` (S10).

## The eighty-five percent rule

**Key:** `wilson2019eighty`. It also belongs to S32 (learner models); here it is read for what it says about "a little harder".

**Read:** in full, from the publisher (open access).

- "For all of these stochastic gradient-descent based learning algorithms, we find that the optimal error rate for training is around 15.87% or, conversely, that the optimal training accuracy is about 85%." (abstract, p. 1).
- The conditions: binary classification, Gaussian noise. "relaxing the assumption that the noise is Gaussian leads to changes in the optimal training accuracy: from 85% for Gaussian, to 82% for Laplacian noise, to 75% for Cauchy noise" (p. 5).
- The link to education, and its limit: "In the Region of Proximal Learning framework, which builds on early work by Piaget and Vygotsky, this optimal difficulty is in a region of difficulty just beyond the person's current ability." "In practice, the optimal difficulty in all of these domains is determined empirically and is often dependent on many factors." (p. 5). The work "remains to be generalized to a broader class of circumstances, such as multi-choice tasks and different learning algorithms" (p. 5). (The quotes leave out the reference numbers after "Vygotsky" and "factors".)

**What paper A takes.** The corridor's upper end, 0.85, and much of its range match the optimal accuracies the paper derives, 75–85 %. The derivation is for learning algorithms, not children, and for two-way choices, not five options. MathTrail's P also includes a floor of 0.2 for guessing [C009], and Wilson et al.'s accuracy has no such floor. The rule is an analogy that supports the choice of the corridor, not evidence that the corridor is optimal for children. The related-work paragraph of paper A now gives the rule with its conditions and calls it an analogy.

## The area's answers

**1. What the research says about teaching problem solving with non-standard problems.**
- Problem solving is a strand of mathematical proficiency. It develops together with understanding and fluency, not instead of them (*Adding It Up*).
- Knowing heuristics by name is not enough. Students need finer strategies, practice at monitoring their own progress, and time (Schoenfeld).
- Metacognitive questioning taught within mathematics helps, including on near-transfer tasks (Schoenfeld, IMPROVE).
- Practice organised by topic can leave knowledge inert, and practice across settings helps abstraction (*How People Learn*).
- Transfer is real within a domain under some conditions. Far transfer to other domains is rarely found (Barnett and Ceci, Sala and Gobet).

**2. What grounds the corridor, "a little harder".** Three arguments, none of them a measurement on children:
- Difficulty helps only when the learner can meet it, and the helpful level depends on the learner (Bjork and Bjork).
- The zone of proximal development, read as support "at the same or a slightly higher level" (van de Pol et al.).
- The 75–85 % optimum derived for gradient-descent learners (Wilson et al.).

They ground a corridor chosen per child and set below certain success. They do not ground the numbers 0.70 and 0.85 for children, nor the choice of the middle, 0.775. Paper A should present the corridor as a design choice in line with this literature. Whether it helps children learn is not something the paper can show.

**3. What grounds the novelty of every task.**
- A nonroutine problem is defined relative to the learner: one whose method the learner does not already know (*Adding It Up*; Schoenfeld's exercises and problems). A task repeated is a task whose method the child has seen.
- One idea in several settings supports abstraction (*How People Learn*).

The literature does not say that every task must be new. The research on spacing and retrieval (Bjork and Bjork) values coming back to what was learned, and MathTrail does come back: topics recur [C019] and traps recur [C020]. So the grounded claim is "new tasks on recurring ideas". MathTrail's check of novelty is textual: trigrams, compared with sketches of up to 200 of the child's past tasks [C027–C029]. It cannot tell a new idea from an old idea in new words.

**4. What grounds the hint as a leading question.**
- Pólya's questions serve solver and teacher alike.
- Schoenfeld coached by three questions.
- IMPROVE trains comprehension, strategy and connection questions.
- In Lester et al.'s design, hints are given "as needed".

These ground the form: a question rather than a step of the solution, and not giving the answer [C074]. MathTrail also lets the hint be the first step [C061], and nothing read grounds that half; how much of the solution a first step gives away is not checked. The review of scaffolding denies the name: a question fixed in advance, not adapted to the child's state and not faded, is not scaffolding. Every study above had a teacher who chose when to ask and what; the service does neither.

**5. Where the evidence is weak or disputed.**
- *Age.* Productive failure favours instruction first for grades 2–5, four of the six grades MathTrail serves (Sinha and Kapur). The spacing literature has little research on children (Bjork and Bjork). IMPROVE and Schoenfeld's courses were with grade 8 and college students.
- *Heuristics without coaching.* No work read shows that solving non-standard problems alone, without teaching of strategies and control, improves problem solving.
- *Transfer.* The thesis that practice on new problems carries over to other new problems is untested for MathTrail, and far transfer is doubtful.
- *The numbers of the corridor* rest on an analogy with machine learning.

**6. What this changes in paper A.** Made in the draft with this task:
- The introduction cites Pólya and Schoenfeld by the checked keys `polya2014solve` and `schoenfeld2016learning`. It speaks of the uneven development *Adding It Up* measured in American 13-year-olds rather than of superficial procedures.
- Neither the abstract nor the introduction claims any longer that olympiad practice teaches the skill. What such practice is known to do is answered in `olympiad-and-measurement.md` (S10), and the introduction now says it.
- The related work gives the eighty-five percent rule its conditions.
- Table 1 states novelty as new tasks on ideas that recur, and says that the check compares text.

Left to the tasks that write those sections:
- The system section (S59) keeps calling the hint a hint, not scaffolding, and says that it may be a first step, which nothing here grounds.
- The discussion (S63) names the evidence missing for grades 1–6.

- **Primary sources not read.** Pólya's full text (only the publisher's sample); Hatano and Inagaki (1986), cited through *How People Learn*; Vygotsky, cited through van de Pol et al. and Wilson et al.; Wood, Bruner and Ross (1976) and Kapur's original papers (2008, 2014), where the publisher refused automated readers or the text was paywalled; Mevarech and Kramarski (1997), paywalled. Each is cited only for what the work read says about it. A work cited directly in paper A has to be read first, at least in the part the citation rests on.
- **Copies.** Four works were read from copies posted outside the publisher, named in their sections: Schoenfeld's manuscript, the Bjorks' manuscript, and the articles of Barnett and Ceci and of Kramarski and Mevarech. Three more come from institutional repositories that hold the published version with its pages: Sinha and Kapur, van de Pol et al., Sala and Gobet. Two manuscripts carry pages of their own: Schoenfeld's do not match the 2016 reprint, and the Bjorks' PDF pages are not the journal's 475–479. A quote from either in paper A needs the published page, which S61 has to look up.
- **A table that disagrees with itself.** In Sinha and Kapur's Table 4 (p. 780) and in the text (p. 786), the estimate for grades 2–5 is −0.09 with an interval of [−0.92, −0.16], which does not contain it; the table prints the undergraduates' estimate outside its interval too. The direction is stated in words on both pages ("was negative", "seemed to benefit from I-PS"), and the notes rest on the words, not the figures.
- **Reports without authors.** Crossref records neither authors nor editors for the two National Research Council reports, so `citecheck add` keys them `anonymous2000people` and `anonymous2001adding` and writes no author. How the reference list names them is for S55. Their editors, named in the notes, are not checked against any registry. Nor would an author field be: `citecheck` compares authors only when the record names some.
- **A question for the product (G6).** *How People Learn* warns that practice labelled by chapter keeps knowledge inert. Whether the task widget will show the task's topic is not specified. If it does, the widget gives the child the cue this warns about. This is recorded in `questions.md` (Q62) as a proposal for the author.
- **Leads for other tasks.** Koedinger and Aleven's "assistance dilemma" (2007), on when a tutor should give help, is a lead for S33. Its copy was on a server whose certificate had expired.
