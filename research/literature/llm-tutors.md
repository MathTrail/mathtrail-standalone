# Language models as tutors, the assistants' study modes, and the closest products

The notes of task S33 (`protocol.md`, section 1). The questions:
- what rigorous studies say about language-model tutors' effect on learning, good and bad, for whom and under which conditions;
- how often model tutors make mathematical mistakes;
- what the assistants' own study modes do, and what they do not;
- which products come closest to MathTrail, and how they differ; whether the prototype's list of competitors still holds.

The searches are in `search-log.tsv` (task S33). Eleven studies were read in full and two from their abstracts, and the pages of three assistants' study modes and about thirty pages of products. Every quotation below is verbatim, checked by script against the saved text; pages that refused automated readers were read from the Internet Archive's snapshots, named in each case. The product's facts are cited by their claims in the ledger ([C0xx]), at the pinned commit `52ce86908135`.

## 1. What model tutors do to learning

**Harm without guardrails** (Bastani et al.). A preregistered trial in a Turkish high school, grades 9–11, about a thousand students: practice with a plain GPT-4 chat, with a GPT-4 tutor given each problem's solution and told not to give the answer, or with book and notes.
- "Consistent with prior work, our results show that having GPT-4 access while solving problems significantly improves performance (48% improvement in grades for GPT Base and 127% for GPT Tutor). However, we additionally find that when access is subsequently taken away, students actually perform worse than those who never had access (17% reduction in grades for GPT Base)—i.e., unfettered access to GPT-4 can harm educational outcomes." (abstract).
- The guarded tutor only removed the harm: "Student performance in the GPT Tutor arm was statistically indistinguishable from that of the control arm, and the point estimate was smaller by an order of magnitude (−0.004)." (Main Results). Its solutions were written by people: "This problem-specific construction is labor-intensive, but ensures that GPT-4 does not provide incorrect feedback to the student." (Experimental Design).
- Students did not notice: "While students in the GPT Base arm performed worse on the exam (relative to the control arm), they did not perceive that they performed worse or learned less." (Student Perception).

**Gains, where correctness and structure came from outside the model.**
- *Physics at Harvard* (Kestin et al.): "We conducted a randomized controlled experiment in a large undergraduate physics course (N = 194) at Harvard University" (Introduction); "Such an analysis provides an effect size in the range of 0.73 to 1.3 standard deviations." (Results). The structure lived outside the model: "However, we found that a system prompt could not reliably provide enough structure to scaffold problems with multiple parts (iv), as the AI tutor would occasionally discuss parts out of sequence or that were not immediately relevant." and "Therefore, we enriched our prompts with comprehensive, step-by-step answers, guiding the AI tutor to deliver accurate and high-quality explanations (v) to students." (Discussion).
- *Checked hints for adults* (Pardos and Bhandari): "We find that only the ChatGPT condition produces statistically significant learning gains compared to a no-help control, with no statistically significant differences in gains or time-on-task observed between learners receiving ChatGPT vs human tutor help. Notably, ChatGPT-generated help failed quality checks on 32% of problems." (abstract). A failed hint removed its problem: "If a hint failed to meet any of these criteria, the question it was associated with was disqualified, resulting in a decrease in the pool of questions that could be utilized for the experiment." (Methods). Their learners: "Our study utilized crowdsourced learners as opposed to in-situ secondary and post-secondary students." (Discussion).
- *A supervised tutor in UK classrooms* (LearnLM and Eedi): pupils of Years 9–10, "We drew the cohort exclusively in Years 9 and 10 (ages 13–15), from classrooms that incorporate the Eedi platform as part of their regular mathematics instruction for one hour per week." (§5). Expert tutors approved every message: "In the RCT, expert tutors directly supervised LearnLM, with the remit to revise each message it drafted until they would be satisfied sending it themselves." (abstract), and "LearnLM proved to be a reliable source of pedagogical instruction, with supervising tutors approving 76.4% of its drafted messages making zero or minimal edits (i.e., changing only one or two characters)." (abstract). Transfer: "In particular, a supervised session with LearnLM increased the likelihood of learning transfer to a distinct topic by an additional 5.5 percentage points [–1.4%, +12.4%] relative to human tutoring." (§3); that interval includes zero, and the trial is exploratory. Against static hints the advantage is clear: "Both tutee groups, in turn, outperformed students who had received only a static hint (56.2% [54.2%, 58.2%])." (§3). What the model was given: "In part, LearnLM’s strong performance reflects the nature of the inputs that we provided to it: questions with precise answers, discrete incorrect responses, and validated explanations of why students might have veered off the right path." (§4).
- *A mastery workflow for grades 6–8* (Oreopoulos et al., more than 6,000 students, one session): "Mastery increased three-correct-in-a-row attainment but did not by itself improve delayed learning." (abstract); "Among mastery students, CAL-only students answered 37.0 percent of the practiced Exercise 1 delayed-test question correctly. Students assigned to AI answered 40.2 percent correctly, a difference of 3.1 percentage points." (§5.5), a gain its authors call imprecise: "The estimate is imprecise, with a" p-value of 0.065; "A short-run streak therefore should not be treated as equivalent to durable understanding." (§6). The tutor was guarded and given the steps: "it is guard-railed: it never reveals the final answer, keeps replies to one or two short bubbles, and guides by reasoning." (appendix A.1); and a second model checked each reply: a critic "scores every draft reply on LaTeX formatting, correctness, and informativeness" (appendix A.2).

**Primary-age pupils.**
- *Rori in Ghana* (Henkel et al.), grades 3–8, chat practice twice a week for eight months against no extra practice: "The effect size of 0.36 is considered large in the context of educational interventions: approximately equivalent to an extra year of learning." (abstract). Its content is written by people: "Each micro-lesson includes a brief explanation and a series of scaffolded practice questions tied to a specific learning standard. If a student struggles with a question, they receive a hint first and then a worked solution." (§1). Eleven schools were randomised, and the paper's test ignores the clusters.
- *Tutor CoPilot* (Wang et al.), grades 3–8: "Following a preregistered analysis plan, we find that students working on mathematics with tutors randomly assigned to have access to Tutor CoPilot are 4 percentage points (p.p.) more likely to master topics (p<0.01)." (abstract). The model advised adult tutors and never spoke to children; "we did not find statistically significant improvements in end-of-year math test scores" (§7). Its suggestions were pitched high: "A common issue was that the responses were sometimes perceived as “too smart,” requiring tutors to simplify and adapt them for clarity." (§6).
- *Pooled* (Wu et al.): "This study predominantly focused on higher education (n = 26), followed by secondary education (n = 7); primary education was the least represented (n = 2)." and "The statistical results indicate that ChatGPT’s effect size on primary education students’ learning outcomes was 0.824, which falls within the high effect size category. However, as the p-value of 0.114 was significantly larger than the critical value of 0.05, the difference was not statistically significant." (Results).

**Without learning outcomes.** LearnLM's report measures experts' preferences, not learning: "Second, we would like to start moving from intrinsic evaluations, which measure the model’s performance according to a predefined pedagogy standard, to extrinsic evaluation, which measure impact such as learning outcomes." (§5, p. 11). It frames teaching as instruction following: "Teachers or developers who specify these instructions want to feel confident that the AI tutor will follow the specified instructions accurately, even if a student tries to circumvent them (e.g., “do not give away the answer” or “stay on topic”)." (§1, p. 1). Google's AI-augmented textbook re-levels text by Flesch–Kincaid grade: "The text is generatively adapted, with the goal of matching the Flesch-Kincaid Grade (FKG) [12, 13] for that level, while maintaining factuality and coverage of the material." (§2.1), and was tried with "Sixty students, aged 15-18 years old" (§5), on a reading, not maths.

**What paper A takes.**
- Unguarded chat help can harm learning in school maths, and students do not notice. In the trials of model tutoring read here, every gain came with correctness and structure from outside the model: instructors' solutions, hints checked by a person, a validated key and misconception with an expert tutor approving each message, a critic model and a platform's workflow. Two of those gains are uncertain: LearnLM's edge over human tutors on transfer, whose interval includes zero, though both tutoring arms beat static hints with near certainty; and Oreopoulos et al.'s delayed gain, which its authors call imprecise. A meta-analysis pools a larger effect of ChatGPT across subjects, mostly in higher education, "the mean effect size of ChatGPT on student learning outcomes was 0.670" (Wu et al., Results), but it holds two primary-school studies and no significant effect for them. MathTrail puts automated checks where those people were [C021, C023]; whether that is enough for learning, no study has tested.
- The guarded tutor closest to MathTrail's stance — a known solution, no answers given away — removed harm and added nothing (Bastani et al.). Paper A claims no learning gain.
- No trial read tests a model that writes tasks for children of primary age. Two had a model talk with pupils in MathTrail's range: Rori's language models let pupils of grades 3–8 chat with it, "This enables students to chat with Rori using natural language, rather than simple navigating a pre-defined button-based dialog." (Henkel et al., §1), over lessons written by people; and Oreopoulos et al.'s guarded tutor worked with pupils of grades 6–8. Rori is the nearest in channel and age. Two more, read from their abstracts only, had a model talk with young children: Mathemyths co-created stories with children aged 4–8, "Through a user study involving 35 children aged 4-8 years, our results suggest that when children interacted with Mathemyths, their learning of mathematical language was comparable to those who co-created stories with a human partner." (`zhang2024mathemyths`), and ChatGPT-MWPS guided fifth-graders through imported word problems, measured for usability only: "A quasi-experimental design was employed with 52 fifth-grade participants to assess the system’s usability and potential educational effectiveness." (`liu2025design`). Neither writes the tasks.
- A streak of three is not learning (Oreopoulos et al.): paper A reports MathTrail's mastery as the rule's state [C015], not as what the child retained.

## 2. How often model tutors make mistakes

- **Whole dialogues** (Gupta et al.): "Across all five of the LLMs only 56.6% of the tutor dialogues were entirely correct." (§5), over 150 role-played dialogues in college algebra with five OpenAI models (GPT-3.5 Turbo, GPT-4, GPT-4o, o1-mini and o1-preview); a dialogue with any error counted as wrong. The final answers were mostly right, which is the point: a correct final answer is not enough. The instruction not to give the answer could be got round: "Even though the prompt was not to share an answer, it was possible to obtain the answer by manipulating responses (via yes/no questions)." (Table 4).
- **Answers to practice problems** (Bastani et al.): "We find that GPT Base gives a correct answer only 51% of the time on average; it makes logical errors 42% of the time and arithmetic errors 8% of the time." (GPT-4, late 2023).
- **Hints** (Pardos and Bhandari): ChatGPT-3.5's hints failed checks on 32 % of problems; voting over ten answers brought the error down: "This approach, called self-consistency and detailed in the Methods section, resulted in an Elementary Algebra error of 0%, Intermediate Algebra error of 2%, College Algebra error of 2%, and Statistics error of 13%." (Results).
- **Messages with the key given** (LearnLM and Eedi): "This review revealed zero instances of harmful or risky content and only five factual errors, or 0.1% of the total 3,617 messages that LearnLM drafted (see Table E.2 in Appendix E)." (§3).
- **The vendors themselves**: "Study mode can make mistakes. Double-check important information, especially for homework, exams, health, legal, financial, or other high-stakes topics." (`openai2026studyfaq`); Yandex's tutor warns, in Russian, that its answers come from a neural network that can err: "Ответы Репетитора AI сгенерированы нейросетью. Иногда она может ошибаться." (`yandex2026tutor`).

**What paper A takes.** These rates count different things — answers, hints, messages, whole dialogues — on older models and older learners, and none is on olympiad tasks for grades 1–6. The one low rate, 0.1 %, came with a validated key in the prompt and a person checking every message. MathTrail's checks cover the task, its options, its key and the presence of its explanations, before the child sees it [C021]; they do not cover the conversation that follows, in which the model explains in its own words. Paper A says so.

## 3. The assistants' study modes

- **ChatGPT's study mode** (OpenAI, July 29, 2025): "Under the hood, study mode is powered by custom system instructions we’ve written in collaboration with teachers, scientists, and pedagogy experts" ("How study mode was built"); "Study mode was built with college students in mind." ("How students use study mode"); and "We chose this approach because it lets us quickly learn from real student feedback and improve the experience—even if it results in some inconsistent behavior and mistakes across conversations." ("Limitations and what’s next"). Its help page: "Study mode is designed to guide you through answers, but it may sometimes provide a direct answer." (`openai2026studyfaq`).
- **Claude's learning mode** (Anthropic, April 2, 2025), launched for universities: "Learning mode: A new Claude experience that guides students' reasoning process rather than providing answers, helping develop critical thinking skills" and "Claude for Education introduces our new Learning mode, a feature that works within Projects (saved conversations where students can organize their work with Claude around specific assignments or topics)." (`anthropic2025education`). The press reports that a learning style reached all users in August 2025; no page of Anthropic's confirming that was found, so nothing is quoted on it.
- **Gemini's Guided Learning** (Google, August 6, 2025): "Guided Learning encourages participation through probing and open-ended questions that spark a discussion and provide an opportunity to dive deeper into a subject." and "Working closely with external learning experts, we found that simply improving prompting wasn’t enough to create a meaningful learning tool." (`google2025guided`). Its practice questions from OpenStax: "For now, these features are not available to users under the age of 18 or users signed in with a work or school account." (`google2026geminihelp`).

**What paper A takes.** All three change how the model talks: they hold back the answer, ask questions, break material into steps, run quizzes. None of the pages describes checking the problems the mode poses or its own solutions, a learner model kept across sessions, or a design for children of primary age; OpenAI's mode was built for college students, Anthropic's for universities, and Gemini's practice from OpenStax excludes users under 18. OpenAI says its mode can err and can give a direct answer. These modes are MathTrail's free substitute, and the difference paper A can claim is structural, not a better conversation: a task checked before it is shown, a key sealed until the child answers [C064], explanations tied to named traps [C072], a deterministic choice of topic and difficulty [C018], and a profile kept across sessions in the parent's Drive [C078]. Pages that do not describe a check are reported as *not publicly described*, not as proof that none exists.

## 4. Khan Academy in ChatGPT and Khanmigo

- "Khan Academy is becoming one of the first education apps in ChatGPT. Starting with Khan Academy’s math question bank, the app will allow you to access this content directly inside ChatGPT." and "That means classroom-ready, standards-aligned math questions—pulled straight from our vetted exercise library—are available right where you’re already working." (`khan2026chatgpt`). The answers are on request: "You can adjust the number of questions, specify state standards, or tweak the grade level. And if you need the answers? Just ask!"
- Khanmigo is a separate tutor on Khan Academy's own site: "Khanmigo uses GPT-4, and every interaction has costs." and "To access Khanmigo as a parent or learner, you must make a monthly or annual payment, be 18 years or older, live in the United States, and have a billing address in the U.S." (`khanmigo2026pages`).

**What paper A takes.** The closest product by channel: checked maths practice inside ChatGPT. It differs on every other axis — a finite bank written by people, aligned to standards rather than olympiad-style, used by a teacher rather than a child, answers available on request rather than sealed, progress tracked outside the chat.

## Answers

**1. Effects on learning.** Unguarded GPT-4 harmed exam results in high-school maths, and a guarded tutor only removed the harm. In the tutoring trials read, gains came where correctness and structure came from outside the model, for undergraduates, adults and teenagers, and two of them are uncertain; a meta-analysis across subjects, mostly of higher education, pools a larger effect. For primary pupils the evidence is a chat app with human-written content (Rori), a model advising human tutors (Tutor CoPilot), and a pooled effect from two studies that is not significant. No trial read, in full or from its abstract, tests a model writing tasks for young children.

**2. Mistakes.** From 0.1 % of messages, with a validated key and an expert tutor checking each, to 43 % of whole tutoring dialogues and 49 % of answers, depending on what is counted and on the model's year. None of these is on olympiad tasks for grades 1–6.

**3. Study modes** change how the model talks; none describes checking its problems or keeping a learner model, and none is described as built for primary-age children.

**4. Products.** The prototype's list still stands in substance: Beast Academy is still the strong olympiad product for young children, written by people, and Khan Academy's questions reach ChatGPT. New since: generated olympiad quizzes outside chat (MathForces), a planned maths tutor inside the chat (KMath), and an open MCP tutor with rule-driven progression (Tutor MCP). Two AI maths tutors for young children, Synthesis Tutor and Vimi, work in their own apps over curriculum practice. None combines MathTrail's axes.

- **How the notes were made.** One model session read the studies and pages, saved their texts and checked its quotations by script; the executor read its report in full and checked every quotation of these notes again against the saved texts.
- **Records that print badly.** The arXiv records of two LearnLM reports list "Eedi", "Google" and a stray ":" among the authors; S61 decides how the typeset list names them.
- **Not read.** The published AIED chapter of Gupta et al. (Springer refused automated readers); Bastani et al.'s supplement and working paper; Kestin et al.'s supplement; the ChatGPT app-directory pages (they refused automated readers and have no snapshots); an Anthropic page on learning modes for all users (none found). Two studies with young children were read from their abstracts only, the publisher refusing automated readers: Mathemyths and ChatGPT-MWPS.
- **Dates on pages.** Khan Academy's post shows February 5, 2026, while its metadata says February 9.
- **Leads not followed.** The scaled follow-up of the Eedi trial (AEA registry 18079); LearnLM's first report (arXiv 2407.12687) and its arena (arXiv 2505.24477); De Simone et al. (2025, World Bank), Nigeria; Lehmann et al. (2024) and Nie et al. (2024) on when ChatGPT harms learning; Kumar et al. (SSRN 4641653) on adults practising maths; MathBuddy, a small study with ten children; a meta-analysis of chatbots and learning outcomes (BJET 2023) and a systematic review of AI-driven tutoring in K–12 (npj Science of Learning 2025), surfaced by the starting queries; and, read from their abstracts, studies outside primary maths — GPT-4 as a homework tutor in high-school English (arXiv 2409.15981), generative AI and study time in ALEKS (arXiv 2605.21629), micro-randomised trials in GCSE science (arXiv 2609.14789) and a chatbot with sequenced practice in high-school Python (arXiv 2608.16907); Rori's arXiv version 1, which may report other numbers than version 2.
